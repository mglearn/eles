Daily openers

60 quick bellringers for the first minutes of class. Students decide, explain, verify, or create something every time, and each opener builds a Student ELE.

No handouts needed: read the prompt aloud or choose Project to show it full-screen, then use the teacher move and look-for on the card. Most need no devices at all.

Robot or Person?

I asked, "What is your favorite food?" One helper said, "Pizza, because my grandma makes it on Fridays!" The other said, "Many people like pizza." Which one was a robot?

  • The first one
  • The second one
  • Not sure yet
Reveal answer

The second one sounds like a robot. A robot does not eat or have a grandma; it guesses words that often go together.

Teacher move
Read both answers twice, then students hold up one finger for the first helper, two for the second, or a fist for not sure. Ask: "What clue helped you decide?"
Look for
Students name a clue, such as "robots don't eat" or "the first one told a real memory."

What Will Robot Guess?

Our pretend robot only ever saw red apples. Now it sees a green apple. What will it guess, and why?

Reveal answer

The robot might say "not an apple" because it learned that apples are red. Robots guess from the patterns they have seen, so showing it more kinds of apples helps.

Teacher move
Partners turn knee to knee and each share a guess and a reason. Call on three pairs, then ask: "How could we help the robot guess better next time?"
Look for
Students say the robot learned from what it saw, and suggest showing it more kinds of apples.
Next
Tonight, find something at home that comes in many colors and tell a grown-up why a robot might get confused. Full activity: Robot Guesses

The Citation to Nowhere

An AI research brief says: "Teens who study with music score 40% higher on memory tests (Harlow & Min, 2021, Journal of Adolescent Learning Science, vol. 14)." It looks polished and specific. Fact or fake?

  • Fact
  • Fake
  • Can't tell until I trace it
Reveal answer

Treat it as fake until traced. The authors, journal, and 40% figure were invented for this opener, which is exactly what a hallucinated citation looks like: real-sounding names, a volume number, a neat statistic. Teacher move
Students vote with fingers, then pairs list the exact steps they would take to trace the citation. Ask: "What would count as proof that this study exists and says this?"
Look for
Students describe concrete tracing steps (search the title and authors, find the journal, read the abstract) rather than judging by how professional it looks.
