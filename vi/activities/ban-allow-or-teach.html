Ban, Allow, or Teach?

A leadership team works the ELE five-question check to craft a defensible stance on generative AI, moving past the headline fight over school AI moratoriums to decide what their own evidence, equity, and families actually require.

In phiếu

Tổng quan

Districts are being pushed to pick a side: some have declared broad AI moratoriums, others are racing to put AI in every classroom. Headlines reward the extremes, but a leader's job is a defensible stance, not a slogan. This session walks a leadership team through the ELE five-question check, vision, evidence, instruction, who's left out, and families, to reason toward a position on generative AI that fits their own context. Teams weigh the real tension in the news (concerns about cognitive offloading and weaker critical thinking against the risk of banning a tool students will use anyway and widening gaps for students without support at home). The outcome is not a predetermined "teach it" answer; a time-boxed, grade-banded moratorium can be the right call for a community, as can structured adoption. What the ELEs require is that the choice be driven by evidence of learning and equity, communicated to families, and revisited, not chosen by whoever is loudest.

Mục tiêu

  • Leaders will apply the five-question check to a generative AI stance decision.
  • Leaders will weigh evidence on both sides of the ban-versus-teach debate without caricaturing either.
  • Leaders will draft a defensible, grade-banded AI stance tied to evidence of learning and equity.
  • Leaders will plan how to communicate the stance to families and when to revisit it.

Học liệu

Trên giấy

  • Handout A: The Five-Question Check, worksheet (1 per team)
  • Handout B: Four Real Stances, a reading of fictional districts' choices (1 per participant)
  • Handout C: Our Draft Stance + Family Message (1 per team)

Trên màn hình

  • Optional: a shared document or slide to capture the draft stance for the full leadership team
  • Optional: existing student-work samples or survey data from your own schools as evidence

Trước khi bắt đầu

  1. Print Handout A and C per team and Handout B per participant.
  2. Gather any local evidence you have (student surveys, work samples, teacher concerns) to ground the discussion in your context.
  3. Seat mixed teams so a single strong opinion doesn't drive the table.
  4. Frame the session honestly: there is no pre-approved answer; a thoughtful moratorium and a thoughtful rollout are both on the table.

Từng bước

  1. 10–8 phút

    Khởi động

    The headline trap

    Open with two real-sounding headlines: a district announcing a sweeping AI moratorium, and another mandating AI in every lesson. Ask tables: "Whose community does each serve, and whose does it fail?" Draw out that both extremes can be defensible or reckless depending on context, and that a leader's accountability is to reasoning and evidence, not to the louder headline.

    Ghi chú cho người điều phốiExpect strong opinions in the room. Validate them, then redirect: the task isn't to win the debate, it's to build a stance you could defend to a skeptical board and a worried parent alike.

  2. 28–20 phút

    Khám phá

    Four real stances

    Teams read Handout B: four fictional districts that chose differently, a K–8 time-boxed moratorium, a high-school "teach it with disclosure" policy, an "allow for busywork, not for graded thinking" rule, and a chaotic no-policy district. For each, teams note what evidence drove it, who it helped, and who it left out. Debrief: none is perfect; each reflects a context and a set of trade-offs.

    Ghi chú cho người điều phốiThe no-policy district matters most: doing nothing is itself a choice, usually the least equitable one, because it leaves teachers and families to improvise alone.

  3. 320–50 phút

    Vận dụng

    Work the five-question check

    Teams work Handout A on their own context. Vision: what is the learning we want, and where could AI help or harm it? Evidence: what will we watch to know, student work, offloading patterns, growth, not tool-usage counts? Instruction: what changes for teachers, and what support do they get? Who's left out: which students gain or lose under each option (no home access, emergent bilinguals, students with disabilities)? Families: how will we explain this so a worried parent understands? Teams must consider at least a moratorium option and a structured-use option before deciding.

    Ghi chú cho người điều phốiPush teams off tool-usage metrics. "80% of teachers used the AI tool" is not evidence of learning. Keep asking: what would show student thinking improved, or didn't?

  4. 450–65 phút

    Sáng tạo

    Draft the stance and the family message

    On Handout C, each team writes a one-paragraph stance (what is allowed, for whom, by grade band, and why), the top two pieces of evidence they'll monitor, and a plain-language message to families, three sentences a parent would actually understand. They set a revisit date. Teams swap drafts and stress-test: could you defend this to the board? To a parent who fears AI is doing their child's thinking? To a teacher who loves it?

    Ghi chú cho người điều phốiThe family message is the integrity check. If a stance can't be explained to families in plain language, it usually isn't clear enough internally either.

  5. 565–75 phút

    Suy ngẫm

    Defensible, not loud

    Each team shares its stance in one minute and names the hardest trade-off they accepted. Close on the ELE commitment: a leader's AI stance is judged not by how bold it sounds but by whether it is driven by evidence of learning, protects the students most likely to be left out, is understood by families, and can be revisited as the evidence changes.

    Ghi chú cho người điều phốiName that revisiting is strength, not weakness. A stance locked forever will be wrong within a year; one with a built-in review date stays honest.

Giấy hay màn hình

Không dùng màn hình

The whole session runs on paper: the five-question worksheet, the printed stance scenarios, and the handwritten draft stance and family message. Leadership reasoning and discussion need no devices.

Kỹ thuật số

Capture the draft stance in a shared document so the full leadership team and board can see and refine it, and attach any local evidence (survey results, work samples). If several campuses meet remotely, each team can work a shared slide and compare stances live.

Có cần màn hình không? This is deliberately unplugged: the work is human judgment and consensus, and paper keeps the five questions, not a tool, at the center. Devices only help afterward, to share and refine the decision.

Bằng chứng học tập

Những gì bạn có thể quan sát hoặc thu thập được nếu hoạt động thành công.

  • Teams answer all five questions for their own context, considering at least a moratorium and a structured-use option.
  • Evidence named is about student learning (work, growth, offloading), not tool-usage counts.
  • The draft stance is grade-banded, names who could be left out and how it protects them, and sets a revisit date.
  • The family message is three plain sentences a worried parent would understand.

Điều chỉnh

Single campus
One team works all five questions for the whole campus; add department heads so the stance reflects different subjects' needs.
Higher Ed
Frame the stance around academic-integrity policy and syllabus-level AI statements, and weigh program accreditation expectations.
Small district
Combine roles so one team covers vision through families; lean on the four-stance reading to borrow options you don't have staff to invent.
Coaches facilitating
Coaches run the five-question check as neutral facilitators, capturing the team's evidence choices to translate into teacher support later.

Mang về lớp học của bạn

Within two weeks, bring the draft stance to the full leadership team and one family representative for feedback, then publish the plain-language version to families in the languages they use. Calendar the revisit date now, and decide the two pieces of evidence you'll collect before then.
