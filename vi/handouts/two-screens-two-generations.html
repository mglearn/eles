Two Screens, Two Generations

A student interviews a parent or grandparent about the screens of their childhood and today, then builds a side-by-side timeline, discovering that every generation worried about a new screen, and finding balance the family can agree on now.

Today's worry is phones and tablets, but it is not the first screen a generation fretted over. Grandparents were once warned that television would ruin their eyes and their grades; their parents worried about radio and comic books. This activity turns a screen-time argument into a family history project. A student interviews a parent or grandparent about the screens of their own childhood: what they had, how much time they got, what the grown-ups worried about, and what turned out fine. Together they build a two-generation timeline and compare it with the student's own screen life. The point is balance and perspective, not dismissal: some worries from the past were overblown, some led to healthy habits like "go play outside," and some of today's concerns, especially around sleep and endless feeds, are genuinely new. Students come away able to listen across generations, weigh a worry fairly, and talk with family about what balance makes sense now.

Phiếu A cho Two Screens, Two Generations

Interview an Elder

Take this home. Interview a parent, grandparent, or older relative (or an older neighbor or mentor). Ask a question, then WAIT and listen. Ask "what else?" to get a story. Say thank you!

warm-up

When you were my age, what screens did you have at home? (TV, radio, a game console, a computer, a phone?)

time

How much screen time did you get? Who decided, and were there rules?

worry

What did the grown-ups worry about with your screens? Did anyone say it was bad for you?

turned out

Looking back, what turned out just fine? What do you wish you had done more or less of?

today

What do you think is really different about screens kids have today compared to yours?

advice

What is one piece of advice about balance you would give me for the screens I have now?

Đáp án và ghi chú

Phiếu A: Interview an Elder

warm-up
Listen for how FEW or how many screens there were, and whether they were shared by the whole family.
time
Listen for rules like "only on weekends" or "after chores," and how they felt about them.
worry
Many will remember being told TV would ruin their eyes or their grades. Notice how similar it sounds to today.
turned out
Listen for the balanced answer: some worries were overblown, and some habits (like playing outside) were worth keeping.
today
Listen for genuinely new things: screens that fit in a pocket, feeds that never end, screens at bedtime.
advice
Write it down exactly. You will bring it to your family on the "Worth Keeping" page.
