Instructional Rounds for Digital Learning

Leadership teams run a full rounds protocol on five written classroom vignettes, noting only what students do with technology and AI, then find patterns and name the next level of work for teachers, coaches, and themselves.

Walkthroughs often end in a verdict ("great tech use!") that tells a teacher nothing and tells a campus even less. Instructional rounds work differently: a team agrees on a problem of practice, gathers descriptive evidence of what students are actually doing, looks for patterns across classrooms, and names the next level of work for the whole system, not for one teacher. In this session, teams practice the protocol on five written vignettes from a fictional middle school whose problem of practice is about students reasoning together with technology and AI. They set their notes beside the campus walkthrough app's summary to see what each kind of data can and can't tell them. Unlike building a look-for rubric, rounds are about learning to see and deciding together what the campus does next.

Phiếu A cho Instructional Rounds for Digital Learning

Coyote Run Middle School: Problem of Practice and Five Classrooms

Coyote Run is fictional. Post each vignette as a separate "classroom." Write only what students and the teacher say and do.

1Problem of practice. Coyote Run Middle School has one device per student and access to a district-approved AI chatbot. Teachers report that students complete digital tasks quickly, but when students use AI or online sources, they rarely question the information, check it, or build on each other's thinking. Our question: When students use technology and AI, how often and in what ways do they reason together and verify information?

2Walkthrough app summary, fall semester (fictional). Visits logged: 48. "Technology in use": checked in 41 visits. "Student engagement": rated High in 36 visits. "AI tool observed": checked in 9 visits. Comment field most often left blank. The app has no field for what students were doing with the technology.

3Vignette 1 · Grade 6 reading (the facilitator models on this one). The teacher projects a chatbot's summary of the class novel's first chapter and asks, "What did it get right, and what did it leave out?" Students write on mini whiteboards for two minutes, then compare with a partner. Six students raise hands to say the summary skips the younger sister. One student asks, "Why did it change her name to Anna?" The teacher writes the question on the board and says they'll come back to it. Pairs add one missing detail each to a class list.

4Vignette 2 · Grade 7 science. Each student has a laptop and a digital worksheet on ecosystems with 10 questions. Most students type a question into a search engine or the AI chatbot and paste an answer into the worksheet. Two students talk about lunch. One student asks a neighbor, "What did you put for number 4?" and copies it. The teacher circulates and checks the completion bar on her own screen. At the end, 24 of 27 worksheets are submitted. No student is heard discussing an answer's source.

5Vignette 3 · Grade 8 U.S. history. Groups of three share one laptop. Roles are posted: Searcher, Skeptic, Scribe. The task is to decide whether a viral photo labeled "students protesting in 1968" is what it claims. One group splits the work so each person searches alone and they don't speak for six minutes. Another group argues: the Skeptic says, "That site sells posters. Why would we trust it?" and the Searcher opens a museum archive. The teacher asks each group, "How do you know?" and waits for an answer. Two groups change their verdict after finding the original caption.

6Vignette 4 · Grade 7 English. Students each asked the AI chatbot for three counterarguments to their essay thesis, then work in pairs to rank the six counterarguments from strongest to weakest and explain the top choice. One pair debates whether a counterargument is "real or just something the AI made up to sound smart." One student in another pair says, "I'll just use all of them," and pastes them into his draft; his partner says nothing. At the bottom of each document is a line: "AI use: I used the chatbot for counterarguments. I kept ___ and changed ___." About half the students have filled it in.

7Vignette 5 · Grade 6 math. No devices for the first 30 minutes. Groups solve a ratio problem on chart paper, then do a gallery walk leaving sticky-note questions on other groups' work ("Why did you double here?"). In the last 10 minutes, each group uses a tablet to record a 30-second explanation of its strategy for a class family page. One student who uses a speech-generating device records her group's explanation; a groupmate holds the tablet. Groups rewatch their video and two re-record after noticing they skipped a step.
