Did the AI Do the Thinking?

Students do one thinking task by hand and a matching one with AI, then take a no-notes challenge that reveals what they actually learned, surfacing cognitive offloading, when a tool helps you think and when it thinks instead of you.

AI can hand you an answer in seconds, which feels like progress but can quietly do the thinking you were supposed to do yourself. This lesson makes that trade visible. Pairs complete two short, similar thinking tasks: one by hand and one where an approved AI does most of the work. Right after, each student faces a short no-notes, no-AI challenge on both topics, and they discover they can usually explain and extend the one they struggled through, but go blank on the one the AI handled. The class names this pattern as cognitive offloading and sorts when offloading is smart (a calculator for arithmetic you already understand) from when it robs you of learning (letting AI reason through something you were trying to learn). The stance is balanced: AI is not banned and not magic. The question students leave with is the one the ELEs ask, what evidence shows the tool improved my learning, not just my output?

Phiếu A cho Did the AI Do the Thinking?

Two Tasks

Họ tênNgày

Do Task 1 with only your brains, notes, and partner. Do Task 2 by letting the approved AI solve and explain it, then copy its answer. Do not improve the AI's work, just let it do the thinking this time.

  1. TASK 1 (by hand): A classmate argues, "Our team lost after we changed our warm-up, so the new warm-up made us lose." Explain, step by step, why this reasoning might be wrong and what else could explain the loss.

  2. TASK 2 (with AI): Ask the AI to explain why this reasoning may be flawed: "Sales went up the month after the new sign went up, so the sign caused the sales." Copy the AI's explanation here.

  3. Quick gut check: which task felt like more effort? Which felt faster?

Phiếu B cho Did the AI Do the Thinking?

The No-Notes Challenge

Họ tênNgày

No notes. No AI. No partner. Just you. This is not graded, it is an experiment on what stuck.

  1. In your own words, explain the thinking error in BOTH arguments from Task 1 and Task 2. Which one is easier to explain now?

  2. NEW ONE: "It rained the day after I washed my car, so washing my car caused the rain." Explain why this reasoning is wrong.

  3. Be honest: which task do you understand better right now, the one you did by hand or the one the AI did? Why?

Smart or Sorry Offload?: thẻ cần phân loại

Cắt rời các dải giấy. Xếp từng dải vào đúng ô trên tấm phân loại và sẵn sàng giải thích lý do.

Using a calculator for long division you already know how to do

Letting AI write your essay about a book you were supposed to read

Using a map app to navigate a city once you can already read a map

Having AI solve the exact kind of math problem you have a test on tomorrow

Asking AI to check your finished reasoning for holes

Letting AI pick your argument and evidence so you don't have to decide

Using spell-check on a lab report

Having AI summarize a chapter so you can skip reading it for discussion

Asking AI to translate a word you don't know in a language you're fluent in

Letting AI do your coding assignment meant to teach you to code

Phiếu C: Smart or Sorry Offload?

MụcXếp loạiLý do
Using a calculator for long division you already know how to doSmart offloadYou mastered the skill; the tool just saves time.
Letting AI write your essay about a book you were supposed to readSorry offloadYou skip the reading and thinking the assignment was built to grow.
Using a map app to navigate a city once you can already read a mapSmart offloadThe underlying skill is there; the tool is convenience.
Having AI solve the exact kind of math problem you have a test on tomorrowSorry offloadYou bypass the practice that builds the skill you'll be tested on.
Asking AI to check your finished reasoning for holesSmart offloadYou did the thinking first; AI is a second set of eyes.
Letting AI pick your argument and evidence so you don't have to decideSorry offloadDeciding and defending a claim is the thinking you're learning to do.
Using spell-check on a lab reportSmart offloadSpelling isn't the skill the report is assessing.
Having AI summarize a chapter so you can skip reading it for discussionSorry offloadYou lose the close reading and can't really join the discussion.
Asking AI to translate a word you don't know in a language you're fluent inSmart offloadA quick lookup of one word, not the core task.
Letting AI do your coding assignment meant to teach you to codeSorry offloadThe struggle of writing and debugging is how you learn to code.
