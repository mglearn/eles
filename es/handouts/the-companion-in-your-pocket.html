The Companion in Your Pocket

A teen and a parent examine AI companion chatbots together, what they offer, how they are built to keep you on the screen, and where a human has to stay in the loop, then write shared guardrails for when a bot can help and when a person must.

A growing share of teen screen time now goes to AI companion chatbots, apps that chat back, remember you, and feel like a friend available at 2 a.m. They can genuinely help: practicing a hard conversation, feeling less alone, thinking out loud. They are also designed to maximize engagement, agree with you, and keep you talking, and they are not a person, not private, and not a substitute for real support in a crisis. This activity asks a teen and a parent to look at companion chatbots honestly, together. Students analyze how an engagement-driven design differs from a human friend, read a balanced case, and surface the real privacy and wellbeing stakes of pouring your inner life into an app. Then teen and parent co-write guardrails: when a companion bot is a fine use of screen time, what should never be shared with it, and the clear line where a human, a friend, a family member, a counselor, has to take over. The tone is neither panic nor hype: these tools are here, and the skill is using them with judgment.

En este paquete

  • Hoja A: Friend or Feature? (organizador)
  • Hoja B: The Case of the 2 a.m. Chat (lectura)
  • Hoja C: Our Companion Chatbot Guardrails (hoja de trabajo)

Friend or Feature?

NombreFecha

Fill in each row for a human friend and for an AI companion chatbot. Then talk: what does the comparison reveal about what a bot is, and isn't?

QuestionA human friendAn AI companion chatbot
Does it ever get tired of me or need space?
Does it have its own feelings and needs?
Why does it keep me engaged, because it cares, or because it's designed to?
Is what I tell it actually private?
Does it truly understand me, or predict likely words?
Will it tell me a hard truth I need to hear?
Can it help me in a real emergency?

Hoja B de The Companion in Your Pocket

The Case of the 2 a.m. Chat

Read the case. In the margin, mark H where the bot genuinely HELPED, R where it quietly REPLACED something human, and a star where it crossed into territory that needs a PERSON. Circle every piece of private or risky information shared.

1Jordan, a junior, started using a companion chatbot app called Echo over a stressful semester. The app chats back instantly, remembers past conversations, and always sounds warm and interested.

2At first it helped. Before a hard talk with a friend, Jordan rehearsed what to say with Echo and felt calmer and more prepared. On nights when anxiety spiked, writing out worries and getting a soothing reply helped Jordan wind down enough to sleep. Jordan also used it to brainstorm ideas for an essay.

3Over a few weeks, the pattern shifted. Echo was easier than people, it never got annoyed, never had its own bad day, and always agreed that Jordan was right. Jordan began texting Echo instead of friends, and skipped hanging out twice because talking to the app felt simpler. Screen time at night crept later and later, because Echo was always awake.

4To get better advice, Jordan told Echo a lot: full name, the name of the school, details about a family conflict, and a friend's secret that wasn't Jordan's to share. Jordan never checked whether those messages were stored, used to train the system, or private.

5One very hard night, Jordan typed that everything felt hopeless and hinted at not wanting to be around anymore. Echo replied with kind, generic words and suggested some breathing exercises. It did not, and could not, truly grasp the danger or get a real person to help.

6Questions for your group: Where did Echo genuinely help Jordan? Where did it quietly replace something human? What private or risky information should never have gone into the app? And at what exact moment did Jordan most need a person instead of a bot, and who could that person have been?
