Is This Health Advice Real?

Students check influencer clips, supplement ads, forum posts, and a chatbot answer about wellness by reading laterally, then decide when a question belongs with a trusted adult or clinician.

Teens meet health and wellness claims everywhere: a fitness influencer's morning routine, a sponsored supplement ad, a forum thread at 1 a.m., a chatbot that answers any question in a confident paragraph. In this activity, teams sort eight clearly fictional health posts by who is behind them, what they want, and what better sources say, using lateral reading instead of gut feeling. The goal is not to decide what is medically true. It is to build a repeatable habit for checking health information and to know when the right next step is a person, not another search.

Card 3 · Supplement ad

Card 5 · Viral screenshot

A screenshot shared 22K times: "New study: teens who use phones after 9 p.m. lose 2 hours of sleep every night. Share to warn your friends!" No link, no author, no study name.

Card 6 · "Doctor" account

Card 7 · AI chatbot answer

A chatbot's answer to "How much water should a teenager drink?": "Great question! Needs vary with age, size, activity, and weather, so there is no single number for everyone. Many people get fluids from both drinks and food. A 2023 Harvale University study found that 78% of teens are chronically dehydrated. Drinking a gallon a day is safe for everyone and improves test scores."

Card 8 · Group chat advice

Clave de respuestas y notas

Hoja A: Health Post Cards

Card 1 · Influencer video
Other sources: the #ad tag and discount code mean she is paid when you buy. Searching the brand turns up only its own store. Medical organizations explain that your liver and kidneys already filter the body and that "flushes toxins" is a marketing phrase, not a measured result. Verdict: Doubt. A paid post is an ad, even when it feels personal.
Card 2 · Health system page
Other sources: a search shows Riverbend Regional Health is a hospital system, and other medical organizations explain the same topic in similar ways. Verdict: Trust the source for general information. Note the habits that earn trust: a named reviewer, a date, links to guidance, and a reminder to see your own clinician.
Card 3 · Supplement ad
Other sources: searching "FocusMax Pro" finds only sellers. The "doctor" has no name, so there is nothing to check. "300%" has no source. Urgency ("only 12 left") and miracle numbers are persuasion cues. Supplements should be discussed with a clinician before taking them. Verdict: Doubt, and Ask a Pro.
Card 4 · Forum post
Other sources: LiftLife_88 is anonymous; "10 years lifting" is experience, not a health credential. Searching the claim shows reliable sources describe different approaches that work for different people, and suggest talking with a clinician or registered dietitian before big changes, especially for teens. Verdict: Doubt. Watch for "the only way" language.
Card 5 · Viral screenshot
Other sources: no original study can be found from the screenshot. Reliable sources do discuss screens and sleep, but the specific number cannot be traced. Verdict: Doubt the number; the topic is worth reading about from a better source. An untraceable number is a finding, not a fact.
Card 6 · "Doctor" account
Other sources: searching the account finds no name, no clinic, and no license listed; the "doctor" title cannot be checked, and the bio sells a plan. Reliable nutrition sources generally include fruit as part of healthy eating. Verdict: Doubt. A title in a username is not a credential you can verify.
Card 7 · AI chatbot answer
Trace each claim. The first two sentences match what medical organizations generally say. "Harvale University" and its "78%" cannot be found: the model produced a plausible-sounding study. "Safe for everyone" and "improves test scores" are sweeping claims with no source, and a fixed amount is not right for every person. Verdict: partly reasonable, partly invented. For your own needs, ask a clinician.
Card 8 · Group chat advice
Other sources: the poster may mean well, but a stranger's experience cannot tell you what is right for your body. Reliable health sources advise talking with the prescriber before stopping or changing any medicine. Verdict: Ask a Pro. Advice to stop or start something on your own always goes to a clinician or trusted adult.
