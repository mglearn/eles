The 70-Minute Mirror

A teen and a parent each track their own real device use for one day, then sit down to compare mirrors, read what the research actually says, and co-write a short family stance on screen time and phones at school.

Resumen

Screen-time conversations between teens and parents often stall because each side pictures the other as the problem. This activity puts a mirror in front of both. For one ordinary day, the teen and a parent each track their own device use, honestly, in the same simple log. Then they trade logs and talk. Before they judge, they read a short, fair summary of what large 2026 studies actually found: some links between heavy use and lower school achievement and more anxiety, a long study that found heavier screen users showed better cognitive processing as teens, and a twin study suggesting the mental-health link may be weaker than headlines claim once family background is accounted for. Students practice telling a real finding from a scary headline, notice that correlation is not causation, and separate the rare adult who studies this from an app trying to keep them scrolling. They finish by co-writing a one-page family stance, including where they land on phones during the school day under laws like Texas HB 1481.

Objetivos

  • Students will track their own device use honestly and compare it with a parent's, naming patterns in both.
  • Students will summarize what mixed 2026 screen-time research does and does not support, distinguishing correlation from causation.
  • Students will tell the difference between a research finding and an alarming headline built on it.
  • Students will co-write a one-page family stance on screen use and school-day phone policy, with reasons.

Materiales

En papel

  • Handout A: One-Day Mirror Log (2 per student, one for the teen and one for the parent)
  • Handout B: What the Research Actually Says (1 per student, a reading)
  • Handout C: Our Family Stance (1 per student)
  • Pens

En pantalla

  • Optional: each person's built-in screen-time or digital-wellbeing dashboard, used only as a private memory aid for their own log
  • Optional: a shared document for families who prefer to type their stance

Antes de empezar

  1. Print two copies of Handout A per student (teen and parent) and one each of B and C.
  2. Read Handout B yourself so you can field questions; it is written to be balanced, not alarmist.
  3. Set the tracking day in advance (a normal weekday works best) and the class debrief for the day after.
  4. Prepare a brief, honest framing: the point is not to prove screens are bad or fine, but to look clearly and decide together.

Paso a paso

  1. 10–6 min

    Enganche

    Guess before you look

    Ask each student to privately write two numbers: how many minutes they think they spend on their phone on a school day, and how many they think a parent spends on their own phone. Then share the real research anchor: a 2026 study in a medical journal found U.S. teens average about 70 minutes of phone use during the school day alone. Ask: "Were your guesses high or low? Would a parent's guess about themselves be any better?" Set up the day's task: stop guessing, start mirroring.

    Nota para el facilitadorKeep the 70-minute figure as a conversation starter, not a verdict. The point is that everyone, teen and adult, misjudges their own use.

  2. 26–16 min

    Explorar

    The one-day mirror (at home)

    Explain the home task. For one normal day, the teen and a parent each keep Handout A: rough blocks of device use, what it was for (connect, create, learn, work, unwind, autopilot), and how they felt right after. A phone's own screen-time dashboard can jog memory, but the log is the record. The rule for both people is the same: honest, no cleanup, no judging yet. The next day they trade logs and read each other's before saying a word.

    Nota para el facilitadorEqual footing is the whole design. If only the teen is tracked, it becomes surveillance; when the parent tracks too, it becomes a shared look.

  3. 316–26 min

    Explorar

    What the research actually says

    In class (or at home before the talk), students read Handout B, a fair, plain-language summary of several 2026 findings that point in different directions. They mark each finding as "suggests screens may hurt," "suggests screens may help or be neutral," or "it's complicated," and underline every place the text says linked to rather than caused. Debrief: "If studies this big disagree, what does that tell us about any single scary headline?"

    Nota para el facilitadorHold the line on correlation versus causation. Students should leave able to say, "Heavier use was linked to lower grades, but the study can't prove the screens caused it."

  4. 426–34 min

    Aplicar

    Headline vs. finding

    Give pairs three mock headlines drawn from the same research (on Handout B's second page) and have them rewrite each as an accurate, non-scary sentence a careful reporter would write. For example, a headline screaming that phones "destroy" teen focus becomes "Study links heavier phone use to slightly lower test scores, but can't show phones are the cause." Discuss who benefits from the scary version and who benefits from the accurate one.

    Nota para el facilitadorThis is the media-literacy core: the same finding can be reported honestly or weaponized. Name that an engagement-driven feed rewards the scary version.

  5. 534–44 min

    Crear

    Our family stance (at home)

    Teen and parent use Handout C to co-write a one-page family stance. It names one pattern each of them saw in their own mirror, one thing each will try, and where the family lands on phones during the school day given laws like Texas HB 1481, with a reason. Both sign. The stance is explicitly a draft they can revise; it is a shared position, not a punishment.

    Nota para el facilitadorPush for symmetry: the parent commits to a change too. A stance where only the teen changes rarely survives, and it misses the point that adults mirror the behavior kids copy.

  6. 644–50 min

    Reflexionar

    Class debrief

    In small groups, students share (only what they are comfortable sharing): "What surprised you in your own mirror? In your parent's? Did the research change where you landed? What did it feel like to decide this together instead of being told?" Capture themes about honest self-tracking and reading research carefully.

    Nota para el facilitadorProtect privacy: no one has to reveal numbers. The transferable skills are self-awareness, reading evidence, and negotiating from shared data.

Papel o pantalla

Sin pantallas

The entire activity works on paper: both people can estimate their device use in blocks from memory without ever opening a dashboard, read the printed research summary, rewrite the headlines, and handwrite the family stance. The honesty of the log matters more than the precision of the minutes.

Digital

Each person may use their phone's built-in screen-time dashboard privately to make their own log more accurate, treating it as a personal memory aid, not something they hand over. Families who prefer can type the stance in a shared document and set the review date as a calendar reminder.

¿Necesita una pantalla? The digital path adds one honest thing paper cannot: a dashboard can show you how far your guess was from your actual use, which is exactly the self-awareness the activity is after. It stays optional and private, because the learning lives in the comparison and conversation, not in the tool.

Evidencia de aprendizaje

Lo que debería poder ver o recopilar si funcionó.

  • Both logs are filled honestly, and the student can name a real pattern in their own use and in the parent's.
  • Students label findings accurately and consistently use "linked to" rather than "caused by" when the study only shows correlation.
  • Rewritten headlines are accurate and noticeably less alarming than the originals, with the student able to say who benefits from each version.
  • The family stance commits both the teen and the parent to a change and states a reasoned position on school-day phones.

Adaptaciones

Grade 9
Shorten the research reading to two findings (one concerning, one reassuring) and focus the stance on a single shared change.
Higher Ed
Add a source check: students look up one claim from Handout B, note the study's size and limits, and discuss what a responsible citation would say.
Emergent bilinguals
Provide Handout B with key research terms (linked, correlation, cause) glossed, and let the family stance be written in the home language.
No participating adult available
Let the student pair with an older sibling, mentor, or coach, or mirror against their own two different days (a school day and a weekend) and still co-write a personal stance.

Reflexionar

  • What did your own mirror show that your guess missed? (Student ELE 4.3: I am aware of my learning strengths and areas for improvement.)
  • When studies this large disagree, how should that change how you read a single screen-time headline? (AI/media ELE 4.1: critical thinking about information.)
  • What made deciding a stance together feel different from being handed a rule? (Student ELE 4.1: I take responsibility for my own learning.)

Lo que sigue

Families revisit the stance on its review date and adjust what did not hold. In the next class, students carry the correlation-versus-causation skill into a media-literacy unit, evaluating how a current news story reports a study, and connect their school-day phone position to the campus policy under HB 1481.
