From Platform Pick to Community Story

A selection committee weighs three fictional math platforms on evidence, assessment data, accessibility, and privacy, then tells families the honest story of the choice in plain language, in more than one language, with a real way to talk back.

Resumen

Choosing a digital curriculum platform is only half the job. The other half is explaining the choice to families who will see it on their child's screen every night. In this session, committees in fictional Laurel Ridge ISD compare three fictional grades 3–8 math platforms using the evidence behind their claims, the kind of assessment data they produce, accessibility, privacy, and AI features. They read a small local pilot honestly, choose, and then replace an overhyped AI-drafted announcement with a plain-language communication plan (newsletter blurb, family FAQ, social post) that says what they know, what they don't know yet, and how families can weigh in, in the district's main home languages.

Objetivos

  • Participants will compare platform options against evidence, assessment-data, accessibility, and privacy criteria and cite the line in each profile behind every rating.
  • Participants will interpret a small local pilot honestly, naming what it can and cannot show, and choose one simple visual or statement to share it with families.
  • Participants will write a plain-language newsletter blurb, family FAQ, and social post that share evidence without overclaiming and invite feedback through at least one non-digital channel.
  • Participants will plan for multilingual access, including human review of any machine or AI translation by a fluent staff member or community partner.

Materiales

En papel

  • Handout A: The Laurel Ridge Platform Packet (1 per person)
  • Handout B: Selection Matrix (1 per committee, enlarged if possible)
  • Handout C: Community Communication Plan (1 per committee)
  • Role cards written on index cards: Curriculum Lead, Accessibility and Special Education, Data Privacy and Technology, Bilingual/ESL Coordinator, Family Representative
  • Chart paper and markers for the communication gallery

En pantalla

  • Optional: one device per committee to draft the communication plan in a shared document
  • Optional: a generative AI chatbot your district approves, projected by the facilitator only, to demonstrate a first-pass translation that a fluent speaker then reviews

Antes de empezar

  1. Print Handouts A–C and make the five role cards for each committee. If your group has fewer than five per table, combine Curriculum Lead with Data Privacy.
  2. Read the platform profiles closely. There is no single right answer: each option has a real strength and a real problem. The goal is a reasoned choice with conditions, not a winner.
  3. Invite at least one bilingual colleague or community partner to review the committees' translated lines in the Create step, or plan to show the translation-review move yourself.
  4. Decide whether committees will share communication plans as a gallery walk (unplugged) or in a shared folder (digital).

Paso a paso

  1. 10–5 min

    Enganche

    The announcement that wrote itself

    Read aloud the AI-drafted announcement at the top of Handout A. Ask: "If you were a parent, what would you believe after reading this? If you were the superintendent, which sentence would worry you most?" Collect two or three answers. Say: "By the end of today, you'll have made the choice and written the version you could defend at a board meeting and a kitchen table."

    Nota para el facilitadorParticipants usually flag "proven," "completely safe," and "every student." Hype is a media literacy problem too; the families reading it deserve the same honesty we ask of students.

  2. 25–25 min

    Explorar

    Read the packet in role

    Hand out role cards. Everyone reads the three platform profiles and the pilot notes on Handout A, but each person reads through their role's lens and marks lines with a symbol: ✓ strength, ✗ concern, ? need to ask. The Curriculum Lead watches instructional fit and evidence; Accessibility watches screen readers, captions, and alternatives; Data Privacy watches data use, retention, and AI training; Bilingual/ESL watches languages for students and families; the Family Representative asks, "What will this look like at my kitchen table at 8 p.m.?"

    Nota para el facilitadorThe Family Representative role matters. Committees without one tend to pick for teacher convenience and discover the family problems after launch.

  3. 325–40 min

    Practicar

    Fill the Selection Matrix

    Committees complete Handout B, rating each option Strong, Mixed, or Weak on each criterion and writing the exact line from the packet that justifies the rating. In the last column, they note what would change our mind: a question for the vendor, a condition in the contract, or a pilot measure.

    Nota para el facilitadorLook for committees that separate the vendor's own study from independent or local evidence, and that recognize an assessment dashboard's mastery percentage is not the same as item-level data a teacher can act on.

  4. 440–50 min

    Aplicar

    Decide, and read the pilot honestly

    Each committee makes a recommendation, with conditions (for example, "Numina, only if the AI chat is off by default and student data is excluded from model training in the contract"). Then they reread the Laurel Ridge pilot notes and write two sentences: what the pilot shows and what it can't show yet. They choose one way to share it with families: a single plain sentence, a simple labeled chart, or a student-work example, and explain why that choice won't mislead.

    Nota para el facilitadorThe pilot is two classrooms for six weeks with no comparison group. It can show that students and teachers could use the platform and what they said about it. It can't show that the platform raised achievement. Saying so builds trust.

  5. 550–75 min

    Crear

    Write the community story

    Using Handout C, committees write a newsletter blurb (about 100 words), a family FAQ (four questions families will really ask, with honest answers), and a social post (under 50 words, with a clear way to learn more). Every piece must say what was chosen and why, one thing the district doesn't know yet, and how families can give feedback, including at least one non-digital channel. Then committees plan languages: which home languages, who writes or reviews each version, and how translation errors will be caught. If a bilingual colleague is present, they review one translated line per committee live.

    Nota para el facilitadorIf a committee uses an AI chatbot for a first-draft translation, a fluent human reviewer is not optional. Watch for mistranslated technical terms like "assessment," "data," and "opt out," which carry weight for families.

  6. 675–85 min

    Análisis

    The kitchen-table read

    Committees post their blurbs and FAQs. Everyone does a gallery walk in role as a family member (a grandparent raising two grandchildren, a parent who works nights, a parent who reads Spanish more comfortably than English, a parent of a child who uses a screen reader, a parent worried about AI). On each poster, they leave one sticky note: "What I understood" and "What I still need to know."

    Nota para el facilitadorThe "still need to know" notes are the most useful feedback of the day. They're exactly the questions that would have flooded the front office after launch.

  7. 785–90 min

    Reflexionar

    The five questions, one more time

    Individually, participants check their committee's plan against the leaders' five questions: What is the vision? What evidence will guide us? What changes in instruction? Who might be left out? How will families understand the change? They circle the weakest answer and write one sentence about how they'd strengthen it before a real rollout.

    Nota para el facilitadorMost committees find "What changes in instruction?" is the weakest. A platform choice communicates a tool; families also want to know what their child's math class will look and feel like.

Papel o pantalla

Sin pantallas

Run the whole session on paper: the packet, the matrix, handwritten communication drafts on chart paper, and a sticky-note gallery walk in family roles. For multilingual drafts, pair committees with bilingual colleagues or invite a community partner, such as a family literacy volunteer, to review translated lines in person.

Digital

Committees draft Handout C in a shared document so the gallery walk becomes comments in role. The facilitator can project an approved AI chatbot to produce a first-pass translation of one blurb, then have a fluent reviewer mark every change they'd make, which models why human review is required. For a real rollout, the same materials go out through the district's newsletter and family messaging tools, with a short feedback form and a paper option at every campus front office.

¿Necesita una pantalla? Drafting in a shared document leaves a record of how the message changed after the in-role feedback, and projecting an AI translation next to a human review shows the translation risk in a way a warning can't. The selection work itself is better on paper, where every rating has to point to a printed line.

Evidencia de aprendizaje

Lo que debería poder ver o recopilar si funcionó.

  • Every rating on Handout B cites a specific line from the packet, and at least one rating distinguishes vendor-reported evidence from local evidence.
  • The committee's summary of the pilot names something it cannot show yet, and the chosen family visual or statement doesn't overstate the result.
  • Communication drafts include one honest unknown, a feedback invitation with a non-digital channel, and a named plan for human review of translations.
  • Gallery sticky notes in family roles get answered or reflected in a revised FAQ before the session ends.

Adaptaciones

Campus-level teams
Swap the math platforms for a campus decision you actually face (a reading intervention tool, an AI feature being switched on in an existing platform) and keep the same matrix and communication plan.
District communications staff
Spend more of the Create step on channel planning: which message goes to which audience, when, and how questions will be answered and tracked.
Short on time
Run a 60-minute version: skip role reading (everyone reads everything), compress the matrix to four criteria, and write only the family FAQ.
Emergent bilingual families
Ask the Bilingual/ESL role to test the FAQ with plain-language checks first. Simpler English translates better and reads better for everyone.
