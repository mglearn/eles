From Values to Guidance: Drafting a Campus AI Statement

Leadership teams start with what they value, sort draft clauses, and write a one-page generative AI guidance statement that people can actually follow.

Overview

Many campuses have AI guidance that is either a ban nobody enforces or a list of tools nobody reads. This session builds guidance the other way around: teams choose the values that matter most for learning on their campus, sort a deck of draft clauses to find which ones are clear, fair, and enforceable, and then draft a one-page statement covering acceptable use, disclosure, privacy, and equity. Along the way they critique an AI-generated draft, which models the same judgment the guidance asks of students and staff.

Objectives

  • Participants will select and define three to four values that will anchor their campus's generative AI guidance.
  • Participants will distinguish clear, enforceable guidance clauses from vague or inequitable ones and explain the difference.
  • Participants will draft a one-page guidance statement with clauses for acceptable use, disclosure, privacy, and equity, each traceable to a stated value.
  • Participants will identify how students, staff, and families will learn about the guidance and how it will be reviewed.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: Values Cards (1 set per team, cut apart)
  • Handout B: Clause Sort (1 sorting mat and 1 set of clause strips per team)
  • Handout C: Guidance Statement Drafting Frame (1 per team, plus 1 per person to take home)
  • Chart paper, markers, sticky dots (3 per person)

On screen

  • Facilitator device and projector
  • A generative AI chatbot your district approves, used by the facilitator only for the AI-draft critique
  • Optional: a shared document per team for the final draft

Before you start

  1. Print and cut Handouts A and B. Keep the clause strips for each team in an envelope.
  2. Before the session, prompt your district-approved chatbot: "Write a one-page generative AI guidance statement for a middle school." Save the output to project. If you have no approved tool, read aloud the AI-draft description in the Model step note instead.
  3. Gather any existing district or campus AI guidance, acceptable use policy, and board policy language so teams can check that their draft doesn't conflict with it.
  4. Post four sheets of chart paper labeled Acceptable Use, Disclosure, Privacy, and Equity.

Step by step

  1. 10–7 min

    Hook

    Would anyone know what to do?

    Read three quick scenarios aloud: (1) a student uses AI to brainstorm essay topics and doesn't mention it; (2) a teacher pastes a student's IEP goals into a chatbot to draft accommodations; (3) a family can't afford the premium version of an AI tool a teacher recommended. For each, ask: "Does our current guidance tell this person what to do?" People answer with thumbs up, sideways, or down.

    Facilitator noteMost rooms go sideways or down on all three. That tells you the need: guidance that answers real situations, not guidance that lists tools.

  2. 27–19 min

    Explore

    Values card sort

    Each team spreads out the twelve values cards. In silence, each person places a sticky dot on the three values that matter most for learning on their campus. Then the team talks: keep four, set aside the rest. For each kept value, the team writes one sentence on chart paper: "On our campus, this means…"

    Facilitator noteListen for values that pull in different directions, like Curiosity and Safety. Don't resolve the tension. Good guidance names it.

  3. 319–33 min

    Practice

    Sort the clauses

    Teams open the Handout B envelope and sort sixteen draft clauses onto the mat by the area each belongs to: Acceptable Use, Disclosure, Privacy, or Equity and Access. Then, within each area, they flip over (or mark with an X) any clause that is vague, unenforceable, or quietly unfair, and rewrite one of them on the back. Compare with the facilitator key.

    Facilitator noteWatch for teams that keep "AI detection software will be used to identify cheating." Ask: what happens to a student falsely flagged? Detection tools are not reliable enough to be the only evidence.

  4. 433–41 min

    Model

    Critique an AI-written draft

    Project the AI-generated guidance statement you prepared. Ask teams: "Which of your four values does this draft honor? Which does it ignore? What would it tell the three people from our opening scenarios?" Mark up the projected draft together, highlighting generic language and missing pieces.

    Facilitator noteUnplugged or no approved tool: describe a typical AI draft aloud. It is usually fluent and well organized, bans "using AI to complete assignments" without defining that, says nothing about staff use or student data, and never mentions cost or access. The lesson is the same: a polished draft is a starting point, not a decision.

  5. 541–61 min

    Create

    Draft the statement

    Teams use Handout C to write a one-page guidance statement. Every clause must trace back to one of their four values, and the draft must answer the three opening scenarios. Teams also fill in how students, staff, and families will learn about the guidance and when it will be reviewed. Post drafts on the wall when finished.

    Facilitator noteEncourage short, plain-language clauses. A good test: could a seventh grader and a parent both explain it back to you?

  6. 661–70 min

    Debrief

    Gallery walk and stress test

    Teams rotate to another team's draft and test it against a new scenario you read aloud: "A student with dyslexia uses AI text-to-speech and an AI outline tool as supports. A classmate says that's cheating." Visitors write one sticky note: what the draft says to do, or what it's missing. Teams return, read their notes, and name one revision.

    Facilitator noteThis scenario tests whether the equity and acceptable-use clauses talk to each other. Guidance that treats all AI use as a single thing fails here.

  7. 770–75 min

    Transfer

    Who sees it next?

    Each team names the next three groups who must review the draft before it is adopted, including at least one student group and one family group, and a date. Each person writes one commitment on the back of Handout C.

    Facilitator noteRemind teams to check their draft against existing board policy and district data privacy agreements before sharing widely.

Paper or screen

Unplugged

Everything except the AI-draft critique runs on paper, and that step works by describing a typical AI draft aloud (see the Model step note) or by printing an AI draft you generated in advance. The values sort, clause sort, gallery walk, and sticky-note stress test are all physical and are the heart of the session.

Digital

Run the values sort with a digital sticky-note board and dot voting, and the clause sort as drag-and-drop cards in a shared slide. Teams draft in a shared document with comment access for the gallery walk. Only the facilitator uses the AI chatbot, projected, so no participant needs an account. In a virtual session, use breakout rooms for teams and have visiting teams leave comments instead of sticky notes.

Does it need a screen? Critiquing a live AI-generated draft makes the point that fluent is not the same as fit for purpose, and it models the verify-and-decide habit the guidance asks of everyone. Shared drafting documents also leave a revision history that shows how feedback changed the statement.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Each clause in the final draft names or clearly connects to one of the team's chosen values.
  • The draft addresses staff use of AI with student data, not only student use.
  • The disclosure clause tells a student what to disclose and how, rather than only prohibiting undisclosed use.
  • The equity clause addresses cost, access, and accommodations, and the draft names who reviews it and when.

Adaptations

Higher Ed
Draft a course- or department-level statement instead, and add a clause area for instructor discretion: what faculty may decide per course and how they must communicate it in the syllabus.
Elementary campuses
Focus the acceptable-use clauses on teacher-driven, projected AI use and staff use, since young students should not hold accounts on consumer AI tools.
Include students
Invite three to five secondary students to join a team for the Create step. Their questions about disclosure are often sharper than the adults'.

Reflect

  • In what ways am I addressing ethical considerations when using AI in learning and teaching? (AI ELE 2.0)
  • How clearly have I communicated our campus's vision for AI, and would a student or parent describe it the same way I would? (Leader ELE 1.0)
  • Which of our values is hardest to honor in practice, and what would we need to change to honor it?
  • How will we know whether this guidance is changing what students and staff actually do?

Take it to your students

Share the draft with one student group and one family group within two weeks and ask them to explain it back in their own words. Revise anything they misread, then bring the draft to your leadership team with the feedback attached.
