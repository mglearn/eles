Does This Need a Screen?

A fast, argument-starting card sort where teachers decide which classroom tasks are best unplugged, which earn their screen time, and where AI adds value, and have to say why.

Overview

The screen-time debate usually gets stuck at "more" or "less." This quick sort replaces that with a better question: for this task and these students, what does a screen add to the evidence of learning, and what does it cost in attention, connection, and wellbeing? Groups sort 14 real-feeling classroom tasks into four piles, argue the close calls, and leave with a five-question check they can use while planning tomorrow. It works well as a staff-meeting opener or the first 20 minutes of a longer session.

Objectives

  • Participants will classify classroom tasks as best unplugged, digital, AI-supported, or "it depends," and justify each choice by the evidence of learning it produces.
  • Participants will name at least one wellbeing or relationship cost of screen use (attention, face-to-face connection, sleep, emotional safety) and weigh it against the learning gain.
  • Participants will apply a five-question screen-time check to one task they plan to teach this week.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: Screen or No Screen? Sort (sorting mat + item strips, 1 set per group, cut apart)
  • Handout B: Five-Question Screen Check (1 per person)
  • Sticky notes

On screen

  • Optional: a shared digital whiteboard with the four categories as columns for virtual groups

Before you start

  1. Print and cut Handout A: one sorting mat and one set of 14 strips per group, shuffled.
  2. Skim the facilitator key. Several items are designed to be contested (especially the oral-history interview, the cell model, and the anonymous poll). The key's answer matters less than the reason given.

Step by step

  1. 10–3 min

    Hook

    Stand on the line

    Mark an imaginary line across the room: one end is "Schools should cut screen time as far as possible," the other is "Screens are how students will live and work; use them freely." Everyone stands where they are. Ask two people from opposite ends for one sentence each. Then say: "Both ends are arguing about amount. For the next 15 minutes, we'll argue about purpose instead."

    Facilitator noteIf standing isn't possible, use a 1–5 finger vote. Don't let this become the debate; it's the warm-up.

  2. 23–12 min

    Explore

    Sort and argue

    Groups sort the 14 strips onto the four-column mat: Unplugged is best, Digital earns its place, AI adds value (with guardrails), It depends: say on what. Rule: before a strip goes down, someone must finish the sentence "The evidence of learning here is ___, and a screen (helps / hurts / doesn't matter) because ___." Anything the group can't agree on goes in "It depends" with a sticky note naming what it depends on.

    Facilitator noteCirculate and ask about wellbeing, not just learning: "What does the screen do to how students feel or connect in this task?" That is Teacher ELE 4.3 in action.

  3. 312–17 min

    Debrief

    The contested strips

    Read the key aloud only for the strips groups fought over. For each, ask one group to argue the key's answer and one to argue against it. Close with: "Notice that the AI column is small, and one AI item belongs in 'unplugged' because AI would do the learning for the student. Where AI belongs, if it belongs at all, is a design decision, not a default."

    Facilitator noteCommon misconception: "digital" means engaging and "unplugged" means rigorous, or vice versa. Neither follows. Keep bringing it back to what students produce.

  4. 417–20 min

    Transfer

    Check one real task

    Each person picks one task from this week's plans and runs it through Handout B. Share with one neighbor: "I'm keeping / dropping / changing the screen in this task because…"

    Facilitator noteIf time is short, have people write their sentence on a sticky note and post it on the way out. Those notes are a quick read of where the staff is.

Paper or screen

Unplugged

The sort is fully unplugged: strips, a mat, and talk. The line-up hook needs only open floor space. Handout B works as a paper planning check teachers can keep in their plan book.

Digital

For virtual or hybrid groups, place the 14 items as sticky notes on a shared digital whiteboard with four columns, and require each note to carry a one-line justification before it moves. The facilitator can then screenshot every group's board and compare where the same item landed, which makes the disagreements visible across the whole room.

Does it need a screen? This one is better unplugged. The learning is in the argument, and physically moving a strip while justifying it keeps attention on the reasoning. The digital whiteboard only earns its place when groups are remote, or when you want to compare every group's sort side by side.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Groups justify placements by naming the evidence of learning ("they have to explain the pattern in the graph"), not the tool's appeal.
  • At least one placement names a wellbeing or relationship factor (eye contact, belonging, attention, anxiety) as part of the reason.
  • Participants leave with one real task changed, kept, or dropped, with a stated reason on Handout B or a sticky note.

Adaptations

K–5 teachers
Swap in items from your grade band (read-alouds, math manipulatives, phonics practice apps, class video calls) and add a fifth question to Handout B: "How will I know young students are still getting enough talk, movement, and hands-on time?"
Secondary teachers
Add items from your content area and a column for "Students choose," with the condition that students must explain their choice. That builds the learning-owner habit the ELEs describe.
Student version (grades 6–12)
Students sort the same strips and then write a class agreement about when devices come out. Their reasons are often sharper than adults'.

Standards connections

Teachers sort tasks by whether a screen adds evidence of learning, preparing to model purposeful tool choice for students.

Reflect

  • How do I decide when technology truly belongs in a lesson? (Teacher ELE 1.1)
  • Which of my routines build emotional connection, and would a screen help or get in the way? (Teacher ELE 4.3)
  • Which newer tools, including AI, am I using because they're available rather than because they improve the evidence of learning?

Take it to your students

For one week, before any device comes out, say the reason aloud to students ("We're using the spreadsheet today because it lets us spend our time explaining the pattern, not drawing the graph"). Notice whether students start asking "Why a screen?" themselves.
