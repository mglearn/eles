Four-Lens App Review

Teams vet three fictional classroom apps through four lenses (learning value, privacy, accessibility, AI transparency) and turn the verdict into a pilot a teacher can actually run.

Overview

Teachers forward coaches a steady stream of "Can we use this?" messages, and more of the tools now include AI features that write, grade, or talk to students. In this protocol, teams review fictional app profiles and policy excerpts through four lenses, cite the exact line behind every score, and practice reading vendor claims laterally rather than taking them at face value. The session ends with a decision and a pilot plan built around the 2026 ELE question: what student evidence will show the tool improved the learning?

Objectives

  • Participants will score an app on learning value, privacy and data, accessibility, and AI transparency, citing specific evidence from the vendor's own materials.
  • Participants will identify at least two vendor claims that need independent verification and name how they would check them.
  • Participants will write a recommendation (approve, pilot with conditions, or decline) that names the student evidence a pilot must produce.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: App Profiles, three fictional tools (1 per team)
  • Handout B: Four-Lens Rubric (2 per team)
  • Handout C: Recommendation and Pilot Plan (1 per team)
  • Chart paper with a 3 × 4 grid (apps × lenses) for the room summary

On screen

  • One device per team for the lateral-reading model, if you have internet access
  • Optional: a shared spreadsheet with the 3 × 4 grid so teams can post scores

Before you start

  1. Print Handout A and assign each team a first app (QuizNova, StoryBloom, or PeerLoop) so all three are covered.
  2. Draw the 3 × 4 grid on chart paper or set up the shared spreadsheet.
  3. Find out which apps your district has already approved and what your district's data privacy agreement process is, so you can connect the protocol to real policy in the debrief.

Step by step

  1. 10–5 min

    Hook

    The email in your inbox

    Read aloud: "Hi! I saw QuizNova at a conference and it writes a whole quiz from any article in ten seconds. Kids love it. Can I start Monday? My whole team wants it too." Ask: "What's your gut reply, and what would you need to know before you hit send?" Collect questions on a sticky-note wall. Say: "Today we turn those questions into a protocol you can use for the next hundred emails."

    Facilitator noteMost questions will be about privacy. Notice whether anyone asks what students will learn. That's the lens most often skipped.

  2. 25–20 min

    Explore

    First review: one app, four lenses

    Each team reads its assigned profile on Handout A and scores it with Handout B. The rule: every score needs a quoted line from the profile, written in the margin. If the profile is silent on a criterion, that silence is scored as Beginning. Teams also star any vendor claim they would want to verify independently.

    Facilitator noteWatch for teams that score on reputation ("everyone uses it") or design ("it looks clean"). Ask: "Which line in the profile tells you that?"

  3. 320–27 min

    Model

    Reading a vendor claim laterally

    Take one starred claim, such as QuizNova's "100% accurate, curriculum-aligned questions" or StoryBloom's "Student Privacy Certified" badge. Think aloud: "Who says so? The vendor. So I leave the page." Show the moves: search the badge's name to learn who issues it and what it requires; look for the vendor's name on your state's or district's list of signed data privacy agreements; ask for sample output and check five questions yourself. With devices, do the searches live on a real certification program you know. Unplugged, narrate what you would search and why.

    Facilitator noteThe lesson for teachers transfers straight to students: a claim on the product's own site is advertising until someone else confirms it.

  4. 427–40 min

    Practice

    Second review and the room grid

    Teams swap to a second app and score it more quickly using the same rule. Then each team posts its scores on the room's 3 × 4 grid. Where two teams scored the same app differently, they meet for two minutes and reconcile using quoted lines.

    Facilitator noteThe disagreements are useful: they usually reveal a criterion that needs a sharper definition in your own district's version of the rubric.

  5. 540–52 min

    Apply

    Decide and design the pilot

    Each team chooses one app and completes Handout C: approve, pilot with conditions, or decline. For a pilot, they name the conditions (for example, "district opts out of AI training on student content"), the teacher and class, and, most importantly, the student evidence that would show the tool improved learning compared with what the teacher does now.

    Facilitator notePush on the evidence box. "Students liked it" and "usage minutes" are not learning evidence. "Students' quiz explanations cite the article" is.

  6. 652–60 min

    Debrief

    What we'd tell the teacher

    Teams read their one-sentence reply to the Hook email. Ask: "Which lens changed your mind the most? Which lens does our district's current approval process skip?" Close by naming the real approval process in your district and where this protocol could feed into it.

    Facilitator noteCoaches are not usually the final approver. The goal is to arrive at the approval conversation with evidence, not opinion.

Paper or screen

Unplugged

The protocol runs fully on paper: the profiles contain everything needed to score, and the Model step works as a narrated think-aloud of the searches you would run. Teams add a "Questions for the vendor" list for anything the profile can't answer, which becomes their next step.

Digital

Teams score in a shared spreadsheet with the four lenses as columns and a comment for each quoted line. Run the lateral-reading model live on a real privacy certification or state data privacy agreement list. In a virtual session, assign one app per breakout room and reconcile scores in the main room.

Does it need a screen? A live lateral search shows how quickly a badge or a "compliant" claim can be checked, or found to be unverifiable, and that speed is what makes the habit stick. A shared scoring sheet also becomes the district's record of why a tool was approved or declined.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Every rubric score is backed by a quoted line or an explicit note that the profile is silent.
  • Teams star vendor claims and name a specific, independent way to verify each one.
  • Pilot plans on Handout C name student learning evidence, not usage or enjoyment.
  • Teams notice at least one of the planted red flags: training AI on student content, an under-13 restriction on a K–2 app, or missing screen reader support.

Adaptations

Librarians
Add a fifth column for content licensing and copyright: who owns what students create in the tool?
Teacher PLCs
Give each teacher the rubric to review a tool they already use. Many discover the privacy lens for the first time.
Students (grades 6–12)
Hand students the StoryBloom or PeerLoop profile and the privacy lens only. Ask: "What would you want your family to know before you sign up?"

Reflect

  • In what ways am I helping teachers meaningfully integrate technology into their teaching? (Coach ELE guiding question)
  • Which of the four lenses do I tend to skip when a teacher is excited about a tool?
  • What would count as evidence that a tool I recommended last year actually improved learning?

Take it to your students

Use Handout B on the next "Can we use this?" request you receive and reply with your quoted evidence and a pilot proposal. Share one completed review with your campus technology or library team.
