Is That Really Our Principal? A Synthetic Media Crisis Tabletop

A fake audio clip of the principal spreads through parent groups on a Friday night, and the leadership team has two hours of game time to verify, respond, and rebuild trust.

Overview

Voice-cloning and video tools make it possible to put convincing words in anyone's mouth, and school leaders are visible, recorded, and trusted, which makes them targets. In this tabletop, a crisis team works through a fictional Friday-night timeline in which a synthetic audio clip of the principal cancels a trip and insults a neighborhood. Teams must separate what they know from what they don't, verify without relying on a detector, communicate with families in the first hour, and plan how to help the whole community get better at spotting the next one. The deepest lesson is that verification is a practice you prepare before the crisis, not a tool you find during it.

Objectives

  • Participants will apply verification moves (provenance, original context, corroboration) to a suspected synthetic media rumor without relying on detection tools.
  • Participants will draft a first family message within a simulated 30-minute window that states what is known, what is not, and where updates will appear.
  • Participants will identify at least three readiness steps (verified channels, response roles, community media literacy) their campus can take before an incident occurs.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: Incident Timeline Cards (1 set per team, cut apart, kept face down in numbered order)
  • Handout B: Verification and Response Log (1 per team, enlarged if possible)
  • Handout C: Synthetic Media Readiness Checklist (1 per person)
  • A visible clock or timer; index cards for drafting messages

On screen

  • Optional: a projector to display the game clock and each inject
  • Optional: a shared document per team for drafting the family message

Before you start

  1. Print and cut Handout A. Stack each team's cards face down in order (Card 1 on top). Print the facilitator notes (card backs) for yourself.
  2. Assign or let teams choose roles: Principal, Assistant Principal, Communications Lead, District Technology Director, Counselor, Band Director, and PTA President. With five people, combine AP with Counselor and Band Director with PTA President.
  3. Find out, before the session, what your district's actual crisis communication protocol says about who sends messages and when law enforcement or legal counsel is involved. You'll reference it in the debrief.
  4. Do not play or create a real synthetic clip of anyone in the room. The scenario works entirely from the written description.

Step by step

  1. 10–6 min

    Hook

    Would you believe it?

    Ask the room: "If a recording of your voice saying something awful showed up in a parent group tonight, how long would it take you to find out? How would you prove it wasn't you?" Take a few answers. Then say: "Tonight is Friday, 6:40 p.m., at Bluebonnet Springs High School. Everyone in this scenario is fictional. The problem is not."

    Facilitator noteSome people will say "I'd just tell everyone it's fake." Hold that thought. The scenario tests whether a denial alone is enough.

  2. 26–31 min

    Explore

    Play the timeline

    Teams flip Timeline Cards 1–8 one at a time on your signal, about every three minutes. Each card is a new development. After every card, the team updates Handout B: What we know (verified), what we don't know, action and owner, message sent (if any). After Card 3, teams must draft a first family message on an index card within the game's 30-minute window (about five real minutes) and hand it to you.

    Facilitator noteWatch for teams that treat the clip's quality as evidence. Ask: "What would make you sure it's fake, other than how it sounds?" Push toward provenance: who posted first, where the principal actually was, whether the original account exists.

  3. 331–43 min

    Debrief

    Read the first messages aloud

    Read two or three teams' first family messages aloud (without team names). Ask: "Does this message say what we know, what we don't, and where to look for updates? Does it repeat the harmful content? Would a family who speaks another language or doesn't use the parent group get it?" Then walk through the facilitator notes for Cards 4 and 6, where most teams make their biggest decisions.

    Facilitator noteStrong first messages are short, calm, do not quote or link the clip, name the official channel, and promise a specific next update. Weak ones either wait for certainty or over-promise ("This is 100% fake") before verification.

  4. 443–51 min

    Model

    Verification without a detector

    Model the SIFT moves on the scenario aloud. Stop: the clip is designed to trigger outrage. Investigate the source: the account that posted first was created that week. Find better coverage: the official district and school channels say nothing about cancellation. Trace: the principal was on camera at the football game at the time the clip says she recorded it; the "security threat" was never reported to anyone. Then say: "Notice none of these moves required knowing whether the audio was synthetic."

    Facilitator noteBe honest with the room: AI detection tools can help but are not reliable enough to be the deciding evidence, and they can be wrong in both directions. Verification of context is stronger than analysis of pixels or sound waves.

  5. 551–63 min

    Apply

    Readiness audit

    Each person completes Handout C for their own campus. Teams then choose the three items with the most "No" marks and write one concrete next step for each: who does it, by when. Include at least one step that builds media literacy for the community, such as a short family session, a student-produced explainer, or a routine for how the school confirms news.

    Facilitator noteThe most common gap is the absence of a well-known, always-used official channel. If families don't know where real news comes from, they can't recognize fake news about the school.

  6. 663–70 min

    Transfer

    The pre-bunk

    Each team drafts a short, calm message for families that could go out before any incident: what the school's official channels are, how the school will always confirm cancellations and emergencies, and a tip for pausing before sharing surprising recordings. Share one aloud.

    Facilitator noteThis is prebunking: telling people in advance what manipulation might look like. It supports a positive digital climate because it gives everyone a shared habit rather than suspicion.

Paper or screen

Unplugged

The whole tabletop runs on printed cards, a paper log, and index cards for messages. Read each card aloud or let teams flip their own on signal. The game clock can be written on a whiteboard and advanced by hand.

Digital

Project the game clock and each timeline card as a slide. Teams keep Handout B in a shared document and draft family messages in a shared space so you can display them side by side. In a virtual session, post cards into breakout rooms at timed intervals. If you want to show what synthetic audio can sound like, use a publicly available educational example rather than creating a clip of a real staff member, and never of someone in the room.

Does it need a screen? This one is stronger unplugged. The learning is in the team's decisions under time pressure, and a real synthetic clip would distract from the main point: verification of context works even when you cannot tell by listening. Digital tools help only to compare teams' messages side by side.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Handout B separates verified facts from assumptions, and the verified column grows through provenance and corroboration rather than judgments about how the clip sounds.
  • First family messages state what is known, what is not, and where the next update will appear, without repeating or linking the harmful content.
  • Teams address the harmed neighborhood directly, not only the canceled trip.
  • Each readiness next step on Handout C has an owner and a date.

Adaptations

Higher Ed
Make the target a dean or university president, the clip an announcement about tuition or a campus closure, and the audience students and their families through social media and student messaging groups.
Elementary campuses
Change Card 2 to a fake video of the principal announcing an early release day, and add a role for the front office staff who field the phone calls.
Communications staff only
Extend the message-drafting step and have teams produce the first message in two languages and a short text-message version.

Standards connections

Leaders verify a fake principal audio clip by provenance and corroboration rather than detectors, preparing to build the source-reliability habits students need.

Reflect

  • How effectively am I using digital communication tools to share accurate information with our community, especially under pressure? (Leader ELE 5.0)
  • If families received a fake announcement tomorrow, would they know where to check?
  • What would it look like to teach our whole community, not just students, to question and verify AI-generated media? (AI ELE 4.0)
  • How do we repair trust with a group of families who heard something hurtful, even after it's shown to be fake?

Take it to your students

Within two weeks, confirm and publicize your campus's official channels for cancellations and emergencies, and add one line to every family newsletter reminding families where real news comes from. Share the pre-bunk message with your communications lead for approval.
