The Five-Question Rollout Check

Leadership teams role-play the planning meeting for a campus-wide platform rollout and discover that the questions that matter most come after "should we buy it?"

Handouts

  • A The Rollout Memo (reading)
  • B Role and Inject Cards (cut-apart cards)
  • C Five-Question Check Organizer (organizer)

Overview

A principal wants every department on a new learning platform with a built-in AI writing-feedback feature, starting in six weeks. In this tabletop, each participant takes a stakeholder role, including an eighth grader who reports that the feedback panel does not work with her screen reader, and the table must reach a rollout decision while timed injects keep arriving. The debrief turns the conversation into the five-question check from the updated ELEs: What is the vision? What evidence will guide us? What changes in instruction? Who might be left out? How will families understand the change? Technology purchases are easy to announce. Rollouts that improve learning start with those five questions.

Objectives

  • Participants will apply the five-question check to a proposed technology initiative and name the missing evidence before a rollout decision.
  • Participants will treat student and family feedback, including accessibility barriers, as implementation evidence rather than a side issue.
  • Participants will draft a vision statement for the initiative that names an instructional problem and the student evidence that would show progress.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: The Rollout Memo (1 per person)
  • Handout B: Role and Inject Cards (1 set per table, cut apart; inject cards kept by the facilitator)
  • Handout C: Five-Question Check Organizer (1 per table, enlarged to 11×17 if possible)
  • Table tents or name cards so roles are visible; a timer

On screen

  • Optional: a shared slide or document per table that mirrors Handout C, so the decision and evidence gaps can be compared across tables
  • Optional: a projector to display the injects as they arrive

Before you start

  1. Print Handout A for everyone and cut Handout B apart. Give each table the nine role cards; keep the three inject cards (Cards 10–12) yourself.
  2. If a table has fewer than nine people, drop roles in this order: Librarian, Counselor, Math Department Chair. Never drop Dani (the student) or the family card.
  3. Read the facilitator notes on the card backs so you can nudge quiet roles into the conversation.
  4. Decide in advance whether you want tables to reach a yes, a no, or a "yes, if" decision. Most rooms land on "yes, if"; that is a fine outcome if the conditions are specific.

Step by step

  1. 10–5 min

    Hook

    The memo everyone has received

    Hand out Handout A and give people three minutes to read it silently. Then ask: "If this memo landed in your inbox Monday, what's the first question you'd ask?" Collect four or five questions aloud without judging them. Say: "Hold those. For the next half hour, you are not yourself. You're the people this memo affects."

    Facilitator noteEarly questions are usually about cost, training time, and logins. That is normal. Notice whether anyone asks about student learning or who might be left out. You'll return to this in the debrief.

  2. 25–12 min

    Explore

    Step into a role

    Deal one role card to each person at the table. Everyone reads their card, then writes on the back of the memo: one thing my role needs from this rollout and one worry my role would raise. Each person introduces their role in one sentence, in character: "I'm Dani, I'm in eighth grade, and I use a screen reader."

    Facilitator noteAssign the Principal card to someone who is not the most senior person at the table. It changes the conversation.

  3. 312–32 min

    Practice

    The planning meeting, with injects

    The Principal runs a 20-minute planning meeting with one goal: decide whether the rollout goes forward on the memo's timeline, changes, or pauses. Stay in role. At minute 5, read Inject 1 (Dani's email) aloud to the room. At minute 10, read Inject 2 (the neighborhood social media post). At minute 15, read Inject 3 (the vendor's reply). Each table must respond to each inject before moving on.

    Facilitator noteWalk the room. If a table rushes past Dani's email, ask the Principal: "What will you tell Dani on Monday?" If a table debates the AI feature only in terms of cheating, ask the Privacy Officer what the vendor does with student writing.

  4. 432–44 min

    Apply

    Run the five-question check

    Step out of role. Each table fills in Handout C together: for each of the five questions, what the memo already answers, what evidence is still missing, and a specific next step with an owner. The table then writes its decision in the bottom row: go, go with conditions, or pause, with the conditions spelled out.

    Facilitator notePush for evidence of learning, not evidence of use. "90% of teachers logged in" is a usage number. "Students revise their drafts more and the revisions are stronger" is a learning claim you could collect evidence for.

  5. 544–54 min

    Debrief

    Compare decisions across tables

    Each table shares its decision and the single missing piece of evidence that most shaped it. Ask the room: "Which role changed your table's mind?" Then ask the Dani players: "Did your table treat your report as evidence or as a complaint?" Finally, return to the Hook questions and ask how many were about learning.

    Facilitator noteName the pattern out loud: the screen-reader report is exactly the kind of feedback a pilot is supposed to surface. A rollout that pauses to fix it is working as designed.

  6. 654–60 min

    Transfer

    Your real initiative

    Each person names one real initiative on their campus (a platform, a device policy, an AI pilot, a new assessment tool). On an index card or the back of Handout C, they write a one-sentence vision that names the instructional problem it solves, and the answer to "Who might be left out?" Pairs swap and ask one follow-up question.

    Facilitator noteIf someone can't name the instructional problem, that is the most useful thing they'll learn today.

Paper or screen

Unplugged

This activity is designed to run on paper. The memo, role cards, injects, and organizer are all printed, and the injects are read aloud by the facilitator. Movement comes from table rotation: in a longer session, have the Dani and Family players rotate to a new table for a second 10-minute meeting and see whether a fresh table responds differently.

Digital

In a virtual session, send role cards privately through chat or breakout room messages and paste injects into each breakout room at the timed intervals. Each table fills a shared copy of Handout C, and the facilitator displays all tables' decisions side by side in the debrief. If your district already uses a platform with an AI feature, you may substitute its name, but keep Dani's accessibility report in the scenario either way.

Does it need a screen? This one is better unplugged: the learning is in the conversation, the role-taking, and the pressure of timed injects. A shared digital organizer earns its place only in the debrief, when seeing every table's decision and evidence gaps side by side reveals patterns across the room.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Handout C names at least one piece of missing evidence about student learning, not only about usage, training, or cost.
  • Tables respond to Dani's screen-reader report with a concrete action (a pause, an alternative, an accessibility review, a follow-up with Dani) and an owner.
  • Decisions labeled "go with conditions" list conditions specific enough that someone could check them in six weeks.
  • Transfer vision statements name an instructional problem rather than a tool.

Adaptations

Higher Ed
Recast the memo as a dean adopting a courseware platform across a college; replace the Math Chair with an adjunct faculty representative and the family card with a first-generation student who works full time.
District leadership teams
Run it across several campuses: each table plays a different campus with a different role list, and the debrief asks what a district-wide decision owes to the campus with the most barriers.
Short on time (35 min)
Use only Inject 1, shorten the meeting to 10 minutes, and have tables answer only questions 2 and 4 on Handout C.

Standards connections

Reflect

  • How clearly have I articulated the instructional problem this initiative solves, and who have I tested that vision with? (Leader ELE 1.0)
  • Whose feedback in our last rollout did we treat as a complaint instead of as evidence?
  • When a tool includes an AI feature, what do families and students need to know about what happens to their work?
  • What will we look at in six weeks to decide whether this change helped students learn?

Take it to your students

Before your next technology rollout meeting, send the five questions to the planning team in advance and ask one student and one family to review the plan. Bring their feedback to the meeting as evidence, and record the decision and its conditions where staff can see them.
