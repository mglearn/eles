Deepfake Election Desk

Student newsroom teams race a deadline to decide which election-week tips are real, which are synthetic, and which they simply can't confirm yet.

Overview

It is three days before the mayoral election in the fictional city of Port Alder, and tips are flooding the newsroom of the Port Alder Ledger: an audio clip, a rally photo, a video, a screenshot. Some are authentic, some are AI-generated or edited, and some cannot be confirmed before deadline. Student teams work as a verification desk, using provenance questions, lateral reading, and source contact rather than gut feel or a single detector, and they must write a publish, hold, or debunk decision they can defend. The big idea: with synthetic media, the most responsible answer is sometimes "we don't know yet," and saying so is part of the job.

Objectives

  • Students will apply provenance and lateral-reading questions to decide whether a piece of election media is authentic, synthetic or manipulated, or unconfirmed.
  • Students will identify the ethical risks of publishing, or ignoring, synthetic media during an election and propose a responsible newsroom response.
  • Students will work in defined newsroom roles to reach and communicate a team decision under time pressure.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: Incoming Tips (1 set per team, cut apart, face down; card backs are the "Reporting Results" printed separately)
  • Handout B: Verification Desk Log (1 per team)
  • Handout C: Editor's Decision Memo rubric (1 per team)
  • Role tents for each team: Editor, Verification Lead, Source Reporter, Standards Editor; a visible countdown timer

On screen

  • One device per team with a browser, for reverse image search and lateral reading on the practice round
  • Optional: a generative AI image tool your district approves, used by the teacher on a projector to show how quickly a fake rally photo can be made

Before you start

  1. Print Handout A. Cut the tip cards apart; print the backs (Reporting Results) as separate slips you hand out only when a team asks the right verification question.
  2. Set up a "sources desk" where you play the people teams call. Read the Reporting Results so you can answer in character.
  3. Make role tents. Assign roles so each team has one student comfortable leading discussion as Editor.
  4. Optional digital warm-up: generate a harmless synthetic image (for example, a crowd at a fictional park festival) with an approved tool so students see how realistic it looks. Never generate images of real people.

Step by step

  1. 10–5 min

    Hook

    Real or made?

    If you prepared a synthetic image, project it next to an ordinary photo from your school's own event. Ask: "Which one was made by AI? How sure are you?" Take a vote. Reveal. Say: "Spotting fakes by looking is getting harder, and looking was never the best method anyway. Today you're journalists. Journalists don't decide by staring. They decide by checking where something came from."

    Facilitator noteWithout a synthetic image, read Tip 1 aloud and ask whether the team would air it. The point is the same: appearance is weak evidence.

  2. 25–15 min

    Model

    The verification desk's four questions

    Teach four questions and write them on the board: 1. Origin: Who first posted it, and when? 2. Corroboration: Does any other independent source confirm it (other recordings, witnesses, official records)? 3. Context: Is it real but from a different time, place, or event? 4. Contact: Can we reach the people shown or the original source? Explain that visual "tells" (strange hands, warped text) can help but are unreliable, and AI detection tools can be wrong in both directions, so a detector result is one clue, never a verdict. Assign roles and review them: the Editor runs the clock and makes the final call; the Verification Lead works origin and context; the Source Reporter makes calls at the sources desk; the Standards Editor asks "What harm happens if we're wrong?"

  3. 315–40 min

    Practice

    Deadline round

    Start a 25-minute countdown. Teams flip tips one at a time. For each, they decide which question to pursue first and request Reporting Results by asking a specific question at the sources desk ("Who first posted the audio?" "Can we reach the candidate's campaign?"). Vague requests ("Is it fake?") get "Our newsroom doesn't know that yet. What would you check?" Teams log every step on Handout B and assign each tip a status: Publish as authentic, Publish a debunk, Hold (unconfirmed), or Don't amplify.

    Facilitator noteGive Reporting Results only for the question asked. A team that never asks about context will not learn that the flood photo is old. That is realistic, and it makes a great debrief.

  4. 440–52 min

    Debrief

    The morning meeting

    Each Editor reports one decision and the evidence that decided it. Compare decisions across teams on Tips 2 and 6, the two most contested. Ask: "Who held a tip that another team published? Who was right, and how would you know?" Then ask the Standards Editors: "What's the harm if we publish a fake? What's the harm if we ignore a real one?" Discuss how a debunk can accidentally spread a fake (repeating the claim in the headline) and how newsrooms reduce that risk.

    Facilitator noteTip 5 (the authentic but embarrassing clip) is the lesson students often miss: the "liar's dividend," where real evidence gets dismissed as fake. Verification protects true stories, too.

  5. 552–65 min

    Create

    The editor's memo

    Each team writes a short Editor's Decision Memo for one tip they held or debunked: what they checked, what they found, what they still don't know, what they will publish (or why they won't), and what they'll tell readers about how they verified it. Teams self-score with Handout C and trade with another team for one piece of feedback.

    Facilitator notePush teams to include a sentence for readers, such as "Here's how we checked," which models transparency.

Paper or screen

Unplugged

Run the whole simulation on paper. The tip cards describe each piece of media in detail, and the Reporting Results slips stand in for reverse image searches, lateral reading, and phone calls. Students still practice choosing which question to ask first, which is the real skill.

Digital

Give each team one device and add a practice tip using an image you know the origin of (for example, a photo from your school's public website) so they practice a real reverse image search and lateral read before the deadline round. Teams can keep Handout B as a shared document and publish their memo to a class "newsroom" page in your LMS. Any AI image generation stays on the teacher's projector, and no one creates synthetic media of real people.

Does it need a screen? A live reverse image search shows students how quickly an old photo's original context can surface, which makes the Context question feel worth asking. The paper round teaches the same judgment; the digital warm-up proves the checks are fast enough to actually do.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Teams ask specific origin, context, or contact questions before assigning a status rather than judging from the description alone.
  • At least one tip is marked Hold with a clear statement of what is still unknown.
  • Standards Editors name a concrete harm of publishing a fake and a concrete harm of dismissing authentic media.
  • Decision memos separate what was checked from what was concluded and include a transparency note for readers.

Adaptations

Grade 9 or first time
Use Tips 1, 3, 4, and 5 only and extend the round to 25 minutes with fewer decisions.
Journalism or broadcast class
Add a second deadline round where teams write the actual headline and first two sentences for their debunk without repeating the false claim in the headline.
Emergent bilinguals
Include Tip 7 (the translated post) and invite multilingual students to lead it; provide a bank of question stems for the sources desk.
Government or civics
Connect to election procedures: how voters can confirm official election information directly with the county elections office.

Reflect

  • How can I identify ethical issues when AI-generated media enters public life, and what solution would I propose? (AI2.2)
  • How did my role help, or slow down, my team's decision? (S3.1)
  • How do I decide whether a source of information is trustworthy when I can't tell by looking? (S2.2)

What comes next

For the next week, students bring in one image, clip, or screenshot they saw online about any current topic, and the class runs the four desk questions on it in five minutes as a warm-up. At home, students can share the four questions with family before the next local election.
