Cart Check: Shopping and Money Scams Online

Before they tap "Buy," students check the storefront, the deal, the reviews, and the payment method, and they practice exactly what to do if a purchase goes wrong.

Overview

Online shopping scams rarely look like scams anymore. A fake storefront can have a polished design, a countdown timer, and hundreds of glowing reviews that an AI tool wrote in minutes. In this activity, students examine a mock storefront, sort real-looking shopping situations into Buy, Walk Away, or Check First, and build a four-part Cart Check: the store, the deal, the reviews, and the payment. They also weigh buy-now-pay-later plans honestly and rehearse the steps to take if they are scammed, because knowing what to do fast matters as much as avoiding the trap.

Objectives

  • Students will apply a four-part Cart Check (store, deal, reviews, payment) to at least eight shopping situations and justify each decision.
  • Students will identify signs of AI-generated or fake reviews and explain why review volume and polish are not evidence of a real store.
  • Students will explain the payment basics that protect buyers, including secure checkout and why no legitimate seller demands gift cards or wire transfers.
  • Students will list, in order, the steps to take if they are scammed and compare the trade-offs of a buy-now-pay-later plan.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: The Kicks Vault Storefront (1 per pair)
  • Handout B: Buy, Walk Away, or Check First? (1 sorting mat and strip set per team, cut apart)
  • Handout C: My Cart Check and Scam Response Plan (1 per student)
  • Highlighters in two colors

On screen

  • One device per pair to practice the store check on fictional search results the teacher prepares, or to look up how their own bank or card reports fraud
  • Optional: a generative AI chatbot your district approves, projected by the teacher only, to generate sample reviews live

Before you start

  1. Print Handout A and cut Handout B into strips (one set per team). Print the Handout B key separately.
  2. Decide how you'll handle the AI review demo. If you have an approved chatbot, test the prompt "Write 5 short five-star reviews for a sneaker store, each in a different voice" so you know what it produces. If not, Handout A's reviews serve as the example.
  3. Remind yourself that the goal is judgment, not fear. Many online stores are legitimate; students should leave able to tell the difference and act quickly if something goes wrong.
  4. Know whether your campus has a financial literacy course or counselor you can point students toward for follow-up questions about credit and payment plans.

Step by step

  1. 10–5 min

    Hook

    The deal of the year

    Hold up Handout A (or project it). Read the banner aloud: "Limited drop: premium sneakers, 80% off. Only 3 pairs left. Offer ends in 09:59." Ask: "Hands up if you'd at least click." Then: "What is this page doing to your brain right now?" Collect answers: urgency, scarcity, a price that feels like luck. Say: "Today you get a checklist that works even when your brain is shouting 'buy.'"

    Facilitator noteKeep it light. Most students have seen deals like this; no one needs to share whether they have been scammed.

  2. 25–15 min

    Explore

    Investigate the storefront

    Pairs annotate Handout A with two highlighters: yellow for anything that builds trust, pink for anything that raises doubt. Prompt them to check four places: the web address, the contact and return information, the reviews, and the payment options. Each pair writes a verdict and their top three clues at the bottom.

    Facilitator noteListen for "it has 1,200 reviews, so it's real." That's the misconception the review step addresses.

  3. 315–23 min

    Model

    Think aloud: the four-part Cart Check

    Walk through Handout A out loud using the four checks. Store: "Who runs this? The address is a misspelled brand-style name, there's no street address or phone, and the 'about' page is vague. I'd search the store's name plus 'reviews' or 'scam' in a new tab." Deal: "80% off brand-new premium shoes, with a timer, is too good to be true." Reviews: "Every review is five stars, posted the same week, in the same upbeat voice, praising 'the product' without saying what it was. That pattern is what AI-written or paid reviews often look like." Payment: "The only options are gift card, wire transfer, or a payment app 'to our manager.' That alone is a hard stop: legitimate stores don't demand gift cards."

    Facilitator noteIf you project an approved chatbot, generate five fake reviews in 20 seconds. The takeaway: reviews are cheap to fake, so check the store elsewhere.

  4. 423–35 min

    Practice

    Sort the situations

    Teams sort the 12 strips from Handout B onto the mat: Buy, Walk Away, or Check First. For each, they must name which part of the Cart Check decided it (store, deal, reviews, or payment). Then reveal the key and discuss the three strips with the most disagreement.

    Facilitator note"Check First" is often the smartest answer. Checking means using a route you trust, such as typing the store's real address yourself or calling the number on the back of your card, never the link or number in the message.

  5. 535–43 min

    Apply

    Pay later, pay more?

    Read strip 11 (the buy-now-pay-later offer) together. On Handout C, students list what the plan makes easier and what it can cost: late fees, several plans stacking up at once, automatic payments that can overdraw an account, and possible effects on credit depending on the provider and your payment history. Ask: "When might a plan like this be a reasonable choice, and what would you need to check first?"

    Facilitator noteAvoid preaching. The honest answer is that it depends on the terms, the total you'd owe, and whether you could make every payment on time. Students should leave knowing to read the terms, not that the tool is always bad.

  6. 643–51 min

    Create

    If it goes wrong

    Pairs put the scam-response steps on Handout C in order and write the exact first sentence they'd say when they call their bank or card issuer. Model the sequence: Stop paying or sending anything. Contact your bank or card issuer using the number on your card or official app. Change passwords you used on that site, and turn on two-step verification. Save evidence: screenshots, receipts, messages. Report it to the platform or marketplace and to your state's consumer protection office. Tell a trusted adult. Watch your accounts for new charges.

    Facilitator noteStress that being scammed is not a personal failure. Scams are built to fool careful people, and reporting quickly can help limit the damage.

  7. 751–55 min

    Reflect

    One check I'll actually use

    Students finish on Handout C: "The one part of the Cart Check I'll do every time, even when I'm in a hurry, is… because…" A few volunteers share.

    Facilitator noteLook for a specific check, like "I'll search the store name plus 'scam' before paying," not "be careful."

Paper or screen

Unplugged

Everything runs on paper. Handout A is the storefront, Handout B is a hands-on sort, and Handout C is the plan. For the lateral store check, give each team a printed "search results" slip from the key describing what a search for the store's name would turn up. The fake-review demo works by comparing Handout A's reviews side by side and naming the patterns.

Digital

Pairs practice the store check by searching the fictional store name (it returns nothing, which is itself a clue) and then look up how their own bank, credit union, or card issuer tells customers to report fraud, noting the official route. In an LMS, post Handout A as an image and run Handout B as a drag-and-drop sort. Only the teacher uses an AI chatbot, on the projector, to show how fast convincing reviews can be generated.

Does it need a screen? Watching an AI tool write a page of believable five-star reviews in seconds makes the lesson stick in a way a printed review never can: students see that review volume proves nothing. Finding their own bank's real fraud-reporting route gives them a step they can use the same day.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Handout A annotations name at least one clue in each of the four areas: store, deal, reviews, payment.
  • Students explain that a large number of polished reviews is not proof of a real store, and describe at least one pattern that suggests AI-written or paid reviews.
  • Every student can state that no legitimate seller demands payment by gift card or wire transfer, and names a safer payment route.
  • Scam-response steps on Handout C are in a sensible order and begin with stopping payment and contacting the bank through a trusted route.

Adaptations

Higher Ed
Add strips on rental listings and textbook resale scams, and extend the payment discussion to how card disputes and chargebacks generally work and why reading the terms matters.
Grades 6–8
Use the storefront and strips 1–8, focus on game currency and in-app purchases, and replace the buy-now-pay-later step with "ask the adult who owns the card."
Emergent bilinguals
Pre-teach deal, review, refund, checkout, and scam with icons, and let pairs annotate Handout A in their strongest language before sharing in English.
Family connection
Send Handout C's Cart Check home and invite students to run it with a family member on a real purchase they are considering.

Standards connections

Reflect

  • What made the fake store convincing, and which check would have caught it fastest?
  • When AI can write a thousand believable reviews, what evidence of a real store should I look for instead?

What comes next

Next lesson, students apply the Cart Check to a sponsored social media ad or a marketplace listing and present their verdict in 60 seconds. At home, they share the "If it goes wrong" steps with someone in their family who shops online.
