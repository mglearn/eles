Two Grade Levels? Vendor Claims vs. Evidence

Leaders trace an AI product's headline result back to the study behind it, read the terms of service line by line, and design the local pilot that would actually answer "Does it work here?"

Overview

"Students improved two grade levels!" is the kind of claim that moves budgets. It is also a claim leaders are rarely taught to trace. In this session, teams read a fictional AI tutoring product's marketing, the efficacy summary behind the headline, and excerpts from its terms of service. They sort statements into strong evidence, weak evidence, and privacy flags, then apply the same moves online to a real product their district is considering. The session ends with a design for a small local pilot, because the question that matters is not whether a product worked somewhere, but whether it improves learning for your students.

Objectives

  • Participants will trace a marketing claim to its source and evaluate the study's design: comparison group, sample, measure, duration, and independence.
  • Participants will identify data-use and privacy provisions in terms of service that affect students, including use of student work to train AI models.
  • Participants will evaluate a real product's published claims and terms using the same criteria.
  • Participants will design a small local pilot with a comparison and a learning measure that would evaluate an AI-enhanced intervention.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: The StepUp AI Tutor Packet (1 per person)
  • Handout B: Evidence or Red Flag? Sort (1 set per team, cut apart)
  • Handout C: Claim-to-Evidence Ledger (1 per team)
  • Highlighters in three colors

On screen

  • One device with a browser per team for researching a real product
  • The name of one AI or instructional product your district is considering or already uses (the facilitator can supply two or three options)

Before you start

  1. Print Handout A for everyone and cut Handout B into strips.
  2. Choose two or three real products your district is considering or using. Locate each vendor's efficacy or research page, privacy policy, and terms of service so teams don't spend the session searching.
  3. If your district has a data privacy agreement process or an approved-tools list, bring a copy of the questions it asks vendors.
  4. Do not present any real vendor as good or bad in advance. Let teams follow the evidence.

Step by step

  1. 10–6 min

    Hook

    The headline

    Project only the headline from Handout A: "Students using StepUp AI Tutor gained two grade levels in reading in one semester!" Ask: "If a board member forwarded you this, what would you need to know before you believed it?" List questions on chart paper.

    Facilitator noteKeep the list. By the end, teams will see how many of their own questions the vendor's materials actually answer.

  2. 26–21 min

    Explore

    Read the packet with three highlighters

    Each person reads Handout A. Highlight claims in yellow, evidence that supports a claim in green, and anything about student data in pink. Teams then compare: which yellow claims have green evidence behind them? What does the pink tell you about where student work goes?

    Facilitator noteThe efficacy summary is the heart of the packet. Look for teams that notice the 40 students, the lack of a comparison group, the vendor-written test, and the fact that the students were already in an intensive intervention.

  3. 321–33 min

    Practice

    Evidence or red flag?

    Teams sort the 14 statements in Handout B into Strong evidence, Weak or missing evidence, and Privacy or data-use flag. For each, they note the question they would ask the vendor. Compare with the facilitator key and discuss any disagreements.

    Facilitator note"FERPA and COPPA compliant" usually gets sorted as strong evidence. It is a claim the vendor makes about itself. Compliance depends on how the tool is used, the district's agreement, and the actual data practices, which your district's privacy review should confirm.

  4. 433–53 min

    Apply

    Now a real product

    Each team takes one real product from your list and, using their devices, finds its efficacy claims, any research it cites, its privacy policy, and its terms of service. They fill Handout C: each claim, what evidence would convince us, what the vendor actually provides, the gap or question, and a local measure. They should read laterally too: what do independent sources say about the product and its research?

    Facilitator noteCoach teams to trace at least one claim all the way to its source. Many "research-based" labels lead to a white paper written by the vendor, a study of a different product, or research on a general practice the product claims to use.

  5. 553–65 min

    Create

    Design the local pilot

    On the back of Handout C, each team sketches a small, honest pilot for their product: which students and for how long, what the comparison is (similar classes without it, or the same students before and after with a clear baseline), what learning measure you'll use that the vendor didn't create, what you'll look for in student work, and what would make you stop. Include who might be left out of the pilot and how families will be informed.

    Facilitator noteA small pilot with a fair comparison and real student work beats a large rollout with only usage data. Usage data tells you students logged in; it can't tell you whether they learned.

  6. 665–75 min

    Debrief

    Back to the headline

    Return to the Hook questions. Ask: "Which of our questions did the vendor answer? Which could only a local pilot answer?" Each team shares its product's biggest gap and one line from its terms of service that surprised them. Close by naming the moves: trace the claim, check the design, read the terms, test it locally.

    Facilitator noteConnect to media literacy: this is lateral reading and claim-tracing applied to procurement. The same moves students use on a viral post work on a vendor's slide deck.

Paper or screen

Unplugged

Run the session with Handouts A and B only, and replace the real-product research with printed copies of one real vendor's research page and privacy policy that you gather in advance. Teams highlight and fill Handout C on paper. The claim-tracing is identical; teams simply can't read laterally beyond what you've printed, so add a note of what independent sources say if you've found any.

Digital

Teams research real products on their devices, reading efficacy pages, cited studies, privacy policies, and terms of service, and searching laterally for independent reviews. Handout C can be a shared spreadsheet so all teams' ledgers are comparable, which makes a useful starting record for your district's review process. In a virtual session, use breakout rooms and a shared document per team.

Does it need a screen? Real vendor materials live online, spread across marketing pages, linked PDFs, and long legal documents, and following those links is the skill. Searching laterally for independent coverage of a product and its study is only possible with live access.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Teams identify at least three weaknesses in the StepUp study design (no comparison group, small sample, vendor-created measure, confounding intervention).
  • Participants flag terms that allow student work to train AI models or allow terms to change without direct notice.
  • Handout C traces at least one real product claim to its source and names what the source actually shows.
  • Pilot designs include a comparison, a learning measure not created by the vendor, and a stop condition.

Adaptations

Higher Ed
Swap StepUp for an AI courseware or tutoring platform marketed to colleges with a claim about course pass rates, and involve institutional research staff in designing the pilot.
Curriculum and instruction teams
Extend the Create step into a full pilot proposal with timeline, training plan, and the student work samples teachers will collect.
Short on time (45 min)
Skip the real-product research; use Handouts A and B, then design the pilot for StepUp itself.

Reflect

  • How am I staying aware of emerging technologies and evaluating their real potential impact on teaching and learning? (Leader ELE 1.3)
  • How can we evaluate the effectiveness of an AI-enhanced intervention in our own context before scaling it? (AI ELE 3.2)
  • What does transparency and accountability look like from a vendor, and what should we require before student data leaves our district?
  • Which of our current tools would pass the test we used today?

Take it to your students

Before your next purchase or renewal decision, send the vendor your team's questions from Handout C and require written answers. Propose one local pilot, with a comparison and a learning measure, instead of a full rollout.
