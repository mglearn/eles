Is This Health Advice Real?

Students check influencer clips, supplement ads, forum posts, and a chatbot answer about wellness by reading laterally, then decide when a question belongs with a trusted adult or clinician.

Overview

Teens meet health and wellness claims everywhere: a fitness influencer's morning routine, a sponsored supplement ad, a forum thread at 1 a.m., a chatbot that answers any question in a confident paragraph. In this activity, teams sort eight clearly fictional health posts by who is behind them, what they want, and what better sources say, using lateral reading instead of gut feeling. The goal is not to decide what is medically true. It is to build a repeatable habit for checking health information and to know when the right next step is a person, not another search.

Objectives

  • Students will apply lateral reading to at least four health or wellness claims and name the move that changed or confirmed their verdict.
  • Students will identify persuasion and profit cues (sponsorship, urgency, miracle language, fake credentials) in health content.
  • Students will trace the claims in a mock AI chatbot health answer and explain why a fluent answer still needs a qualified source.
  • Students will write a personal plan naming when they would take a health question to a trusted adult, school nurse, or clinician.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: Health Post Cards (1 set per team, cut apart; print the card backs separately as the facilitator key and "Other sources" slips)
  • Handout B: Health Claim Check Log (1 per team)
  • Handout C: My Health Info Game Plan (1 per student)
  • Sticky notes in three colors, or index cards labeled Trust · Doubt · Ask a Pro

On screen

  • One device with a browser per team for the lateral-reading round
  • Optional: a generative AI chatbot your district approves, projected by the teacher only, for the live comparison in the Debrief

Before you start

  1. Print and cut Handout A. Keep the backs as your key and cut the "Other sources" text into slips for the unplugged path.
  2. Read every card back. Your job is to coach the checking moves, not to rule on the health question. If a student asks "so what's the right answer?", the answer is "a qualified source, and for your own body, a clinician."
  3. Find out who your school nurse and counselor are and how students reach them, so you can name them in the Apply step.
  4. Be ready for personal disclosures. Some students have real health worries. Remind the class that no one shares their own health details today, and follow up privately if a student seems concerned.

Step by step

  1. 10–5 min

    Hook

    Would you try it?

    Read Card 1 aloud (the "Glow Reset" tea influencer). Ask: "Would you try it, skip it, or want to know more first?" Students vote with a hand signal. Then ask: "What made you lean that way: the person, the before-and-after, the comments?" List a few reasons on the board without judging them. Say: "Health posts are designed to feel personal and urgent. Today you'll learn the checking moves that work even when a post feels convincing."

    Facilitator noteMake it clear up front: every person, product, and site on the cards is invented. We are practicing moves, not reviewing real products.

  2. 25–15 min

    Explore

    Round 1: judge it from the post alone

    Teams draw Cards 2–5 and may use only what is on the card. For each, they log on Handout B: What is the claim? Who is behind it? What do they want me to do (buy, share, stop, worry)? Verdict: Trust, Doubt, or Ask a Pro, with a confidence score from 1–5.

    Facilitator noteListen for "she's a nurse, so it's true" or "it has lots of five-star reviews." Those are the vertical-reading habits you'll revisit.

  3. 315–22 min

    Model

    Think aloud: three questions for health info

    Model on Card 3 (the supplement ad). Narrate three moves out loud: 1. Who's behind it? Search the brand or person's name plus words like "sponsored" or "who owns." 2. What do better sources say? Search the claim itself and look for hospitals, university health centers, public health agencies, or medical organizations that explain the topic, with a named reviewer and a date. 3. Is this a question for a person? If the post asks you to change what you eat, take, or skip for your own body, the next step is a trusted adult or clinician. Point out what you do not do: trust the testimonials, the lab coat, or the number of followers.

    Facilitator noteUnplugged: read the "Other sources" slip aloud as though it were your search results. The move of leaving the post to check elsewhere is the same.

  4. 422–34 min

    Practice

    Round 2: read laterally, including the chatbot

    Teams re-check Cards 2–5 and take on Cards 6–8, with roles rotating each card: Searcher (drives the device or asks for the slip), Skeptic (asks "who profits?"), Scribe (logs moves on Handout B). Card 7 is a mock AI chatbot answer. Teams must pull out each factual claim in it and mark which ones they could trace to a reliable source and which they could not.

    Facilitator noteMost teams find the chatbot answer is calm and partly sensible, with one invented statistic and a made-up "study." The lesson: an AI answer has no author to investigate, so tracing claims is the only check, and a chatbot is not a clinician.

  5. 534–41 min

    Debrief

    What flipped, and what belongs with a person?

    Compare Round 1 and Round 2 confidence scores. Ask: "Which cards flipped? Which move flipped them?" Then sort the cards on the board into three columns: Trust the source, Doubt it, Take it to a person. Ask: "Why does Card 8 (the forum post about stopping a prescription) go in the last column even if the poster means well?" Build a class list: Signs a health post needs a second look: selling something, miracle or guaranteed results, a short timeline, fake or vague credentials, "doctors don't want you to know," no date or reviewer, and advice to stop or start something on your own.

    Facilitator noteIf you project an approved chatbot, ask it a general wellness question and have students spot whether it tells them to see a clinician, cites sources, or states numbers without a trace. Don't enter any personal health details.

  6. 641–47 min

    Apply

    My game plan

    Each student completes Handout C: two go-to kinds of reliable sources, the three questions they'll ask of any health post, and the trusted people they could take a question to (a family member, school nurse, counselor, doctor or clinic). Name your school nurse and counselor and how to reach them.

    Facilitator noteHandout C stays private. Students write roles or initials, not personal health details.

  7. 747–50 min

    Reflect

    Exit ticket

    On a sticky note, students finish: "The next time a health post makes me want to buy, stop, or worry, I will first…" Collect them as you would an exit ticket.

    Facilitator noteLook for a named move (search the source, find better coverage, trace the claim) or a named person, not "be careful."

Paper or screen

Unplugged

Run both rounds on paper. The card backs include an "Other sources" note describing what a lateral search would turn up. When a team asks "what do other sources say?", hand them that slip. Asking for the slip is the visible habit you want. Model the chatbot card by reading it aloud as a printed AI answer, and have teams underline each claim before you hand them the trace slip.

Digital

Give each team one device for Round 2 only, so Round 1 stays a pure read of the post itself. Teams search the fictional names and discover they return nothing, then search the claim to find reliable health organizations explaining the topic. In an LMS, post the cards as a slide deck and have scribes fill Handout B as a shared table. Only the teacher runs any AI chatbot, on the projector, with no personal information entered.

Does it need a screen? Live searching shows students how quickly a lateral check turns up a sponsor, a missing credential, or a better explanation from a medical organization, and how a chatbot answer can sound right while inventing a number. That speed and contrast is the evidence of learning paper can only describe.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Handout B shows at least one changed verdict that names the move responsible, such as "Searched the brand: owned by the company selling it."
  • Teams break the chatbot answer into separate claims and mark each as traced or untraceable instead of trusting or rejecting it whole.
  • Students place advice to start, stop, or change something for their own body in the "take it to a person" column and explain why.
  • Exit tickets name a specific checking move or trusted person.

Adaptations

Grades 6–8
Use Cards 1, 3, 7, and 8 and shrink the moves to two: "Who is selling or sharing this?" and "Who could I ask?" Make the trusted-adult plan the main product.
Health science or CTE classes
Add a round comparing two reliable sources on the same topic and discuss why a reviewer's name, a review date, and cited guidance matter in patient education materials.
Emergent bilinguals
Pre-teach claim, sponsor, credential, and clinician with visuals, and let teams search in their strongest language; many health organizations publish reliable information in several languages.
Sensitive topics
If your class includes students managing a health condition, keep all cards general, never ask for examples from students' lives, and point to the nurse and counselor privately.

Reflect

  • How do I know if a health source online is trustworthy, and what move did I use to find out? (Student ELE S2.2)
  • What did the chatbot answer get right, what did it invent, and how did I tell the difference?
  • Which health questions would I rather take to a person than a search bar, and why?

What comes next

In the next lesson, students bring one health or wellness claim they have seen (without personal details) and run it through the three questions for a partner. At home, they can share the class list of "signs a health post needs a second look" with a family member.
