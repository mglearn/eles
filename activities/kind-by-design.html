Kind by Design

Teachers rewrite prickly class messages, co-create norms for digital spaces the way students would, and work through what to do when a student's closest confidant is an AI companion chatbot.

Overview

Relationships don't pause when learning moves online. Tone disappears in text, group chats and discussion boards can turn sharp fast, and some students now turn to AI "companion" chatbots for comfort and conversation. In this unplugged session, teachers first do what students would do: rewrite real-feeling class messages so they're kind and clear, and build a norms charter together. Then they work through four scenarios about students and companion chatbots, focusing on what the student needs, what a chatbot can and can't give, and the teacher move that keeps a human relationship at the center.

Objectives

  • Participants will rewrite digital messages to be both kind and clear, and name why text-only communication is easy to misread.
  • Participants will use a student-centered process to co-create norms for a digital learning space, including what happens when a norm is broken.
  • Participants will plan a caring, non-judgmental response when a student relies on an AI companion chatbot, including when to involve a counselor or follow district procedures.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: Message Makeover Cards (1 set per team, cut apart; backs printed as a facilitator key)
  • Handout B: Companion Chatbot Scenarios (1 per person)
  • Handout C: Our Digital Norms Charter (1 per team, enlarged if possible)
  • Sticky notes in two colors; dot stickers; chart paper

On screen

  • Optional: your LMS discussion board or class chat tool, projected, so teams can see where their norms will live
  • Optional: an anonymous digital board for the silent brainstorm in larger rooms

Before you start

  1. Print and cut Handout A. Print the card backs separately for yourself.
  2. Find out your district's procedures for when a student discloses distress or safety concerns, and the name of your campus counselor contact. Put both on a slide for the scenarios step.
  3. Read Handout B. Scenario 2 includes a student who hints at wanting to quit everything; be ready to say clearly that this triggers your district's procedures, not a teacher's judgment call.

Step by step

  1. 10–5 min

    Hook

    Same words, three tones

    Read Card 1 ("Fine. Do it your way.") aloud three ways: cheerful, flat, and annoyed. Ask: "Which one did the student mean?" There's no way to know. Say: "In text, the reader supplies the tone, usually the worst one when they're anxious. Every student in a digital class is reading messages without a voice or a face. Today we design for that."

    Facilitator noteInvite one participant to read it a fourth way. The laughter helps, and it proves the point.

  2. 25–15 min

    Explore

    Message makeover, as students

    Teams draw Cards 2–8 and, acting as students, rewrite each message so it's kind and clear: it keeps the real point (disagreement, a deadline, a concern) and removes what could sting. Use the peer-feedback frame on the cards where it fits: "I noticed… I wonder… One idea…" Teams read their best rewrite aloud, then compare with the key.

    Facilitator noteWatch for rewrites that become so gentle the message disappears. Kind is not the same as vague; Card 5 especially needs to stay honest.

  3. 315–25 min

    Create

    Co-create the norms charter

    Model the student process. Silent brainstorm (2 minutes): each person writes "In our online class space, I want to feel…" on one color of sticky note and "…so we will…" on the other. Cluster (3 minutes): teams group similar notes. Name and vote (2 minutes): each person places three dots; the top three or four become norms. Fill Handout C (3 minutes): for each norm, what it looks like online, sounds like in writing, and what we do when it's broken.

    Facilitator noteThe last column matters most. Norms without a repair plan become rules students watch others break. Push for restorative answers ("we message privately first," "we can ask for a restart") over punishments.

  4. 425–38 min

    Explore

    When the confidant is a chatbot

    Teams read the four scenarios on Handout B. For each, they answer three questions on sticky notes: What does this student need? What can a chatbot give, and what can't it give? What's my move as the teacher? Say before they start: "We're not here to shame students or technology. Many students find these chats comforting. Our job is to make sure no student's only close relationship is with a program designed by a company."

    Facilitator noteKey ideas to surface: a chatbot generates text; it doesn't feel sadness or care, even when it says so. Some apps may be designed to keep users engaged. And any hint of self-harm or danger, whether said to a person or found in a chat, goes through district procedures and the counselor right away.

  5. 538–45 min

    Debrief

    Step out of role

    As teachers now, discuss: "Which of today's student experiences would build relationships in your class? Where do students in your class practice disagreeing kindly? Which student came to mind during the scenarios, and what's one small thing you'll do this week to strengthen your connection with them?" Share your district's procedures and counselor contact on the slide.

    Facilitator noteKeep the last question private if the group prefers. It's the most important one.

  6. 645–50 min

    Transfer

    Plan the first 15 minutes

    Each person writes on a sticky note how they'll run the norms charter with students in the first 15 minutes of a class period this week or next, and which message makeover card they'll adapt. Post notes on the way out.

    Facilitator noteTeachers of younger students may prefer to do the message makeover with pictures and speech bubbles; that works well.

Paper or screen

Unplugged

The whole session is designed to run unplugged: cards, sticky notes, chart paper, and conversation. The same is true for students. The norms can be written on paper first and posted later wherever the class communicates online.

Digital

Post the finished norms charter at the top of the LMS discussion board or class chat, and have students revisit it after the first two weeks of online discussion. For large or virtual groups, run the silent brainstorm on an anonymous digital board and the message makeover in breakout rooms with a shared document. The companion chatbot scenarios should stay discussion-based; there's no need to demo a companion app.

Does it need a screen? This one is better unplugged. Kind communication and trust are built face to face first, and the session models that. Digital tools earn a place afterward, when the charter moves to where students actually communicate so it can be referenced in the moment.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Rewritten messages keep the real point (disagreement, concern, deadline) while removing sarcasm, blame, or all caps.
  • Each norm on Handout C has a concrete repair action in the last column, not just a consequence.
  • Scenario responses separate what the student needs from what the chatbot offers, and name the counselor or district procedure for Scenario 2.
  • Participants name a specific first step for using the charter with their students.

Adaptations

Grades 3–5 teachers
Use Cards 1, 2, 4, and 6 with speech-bubble rewrites, and adapt Scenario 3 as a read-aloud: "Can a computer really be sad?" Keep norms to three, in students' own words.
High school teachers
Add a norm about AI use in discussion boards (for example, "If AI helped write my post, I say so") and let students debate Scenario 4 as a case study.
Librarians
Adapt the charter for library chat, book clubs, or makerspace group work, and keep a set of message makeover cards at the circulation desk for student helpers.
Counselor partnership
Co-facilitate the scenarios step with a campus counselor, who can explain referral steps and how teachers can support students without acting as therapists.

Reflect

  • How do I foster positive relationships with students when some of our communication happens through screens? (Teacher ELE 4.1)
  • Where do my students practice disagreeing kindly and giving feedback to peers? (Teacher ELE 4.2)
  • How will I notice if a student is relying on a chatbot for emotional support, and what will I do? (Teacher ELE 4.3)

Take it to your students

Run the norms charter with one class this week and post it where students communicate online. After two weeks, have students rate how well the class is living each norm and choose one to strengthen. Keep a few before-and-after student messages as evidence.
