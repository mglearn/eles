Real, Pretend, or Changed?

Young detectives move, wiggle, or freeze to decide whether a picture or story is real, pretend on purpose, or changed to look real, and learn to ask a grown-up when they can't tell.

Overview

Pretend is wonderful. Stories, cartoons, and drawings are made up on purpose, and everyone knows it. The tricky kind is a picture that has been changed, or made by a computer, so it looks real. In this game, you describe pictures and stories aloud, and children stand tall for Real, wiggle for Pretend, or freeze like a statue for Changed. They practice three detective questions and learn the most important move of all: when you can't tell by looking, ask a grown-up and check.

Objectives

  • Students will sort described pictures and stories into real, pretend on purpose, and changed or computer-made, and give a clue for one choice.
  • Students will use detective questions (Could this really happen? Who made it? Does something look strange?) to explain their thinking to a partner.
  • Students will explain that computers can now make pictures that look like real photos, so looking alone is not always enough.
  • Students will name a grown-up they can ask to help check.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: Real, Pretend, or Changed? sort strips (1 teacher set, cut apart)
  • Handout B: Clue Detective Checklist (1 per pair, or 1 enlarged for the board)
  • Handout C: My Detective Page (1 per student)
  • Three signs on the board or wall: REAL (stand tall), PRETEND (wiggle), CHANGED (freeze); crayons

On screen

  • Optional: an AI image generator your district approves, run by the teacher on a projector
  • Optional: a real photo you took of your classroom or playground, shown on the projector

Before you start

  1. Cut apart the 12 strips from Handout A. Put them in the order listed; the first four are the easiest.
  2. Post the three signs with a stick-figure pose drawn next to each.
  3. Print or enlarge Handout B so the three detective questions are visible to everyone.
  4. If you'll do the projected demo, try your district-approved image generator ahead of time with the prompt in the Digital section, and take one real photo of your classroom.

Step by step

  1. 10–4 min

    Hook

    "I saw a dog as tall as a house!"

    Say with great excitement: "On my way to school I saw a dog as tall as a house!" Pause. Ask: "Do you believe me? Thumbs up or thumbs down." Then: "What if I showed you a picture of it? Would you believe it then?" Let children talk. Say: "Today you're detectives. Some things are real, some are pretend, and some are changed to trick our eyes."

    Facilitator noteMany children will say a picture would prove it. Hold on to that; the lesson will show that pictures can be changed.

  2. 24–9 min

    Model

    Three kinds, three poses, three questions

    Teach the poses: REAL means it really happened or really exists (stand tall). PRETEND means made up on purpose, like a story or a cartoon, and that's OK (wiggle). CHANGED means someone changed a real picture, or a computer made it, so it looks real but isn't (freeze like a statue). Then read the three detective questions on Handout B aloud: "Could this really happen? Who made it? Does something look strange?" Model one strip, thinking aloud with all three questions.

    Facilitator noteThe key difference between pretend and changed: pretend doesn't try to fool you. Changed does, or at least it looks real when it isn't.

  3. 39–19 min

    Practice

    Detective moves

    Read each strip aloud, slowly. Children show the pose. Pick one child: "What clue helped you?" Then tape the strip under the matching sign. Every third strip, have partners turn and tell each other their clue using the frame: "I think it's ___ because ___." For strips like the lion in the kitchen, ask: "If you really weren't sure, who could you ask?"

    Facilitator noteExpect disagreement on some strips. That's healthy. Ask both sides for their clue and remind them that detectives explain their thinking.

  4. 419–24 min

    Explore

    How does a picture get changed?

    Hold up a child's drawing (with permission) or a simple drawing you made of a house. Say: "This is a real drawing of my house." Tape a paper cut-out of a giant cat on the roof. "Now it's changed. If I took a photo of it, would it look real?" Then explain simply: "Some computers use AI to make pictures. You type words, and the computer makes a picture that can look just like a real photo. So sometimes our eyes can't tell. That's why we ask a grown-up and check."

    Facilitator noteAvoid telling children they can always spot fakes by looking for extra fingers or blurry spots. Those clues sometimes help, but computer-made pictures keep getting better. Checking with a trusted grown-up and a trusted source is the lasting habit.

  5. 524–30 min

    Reflect

    My Detective Page

    Children complete Handout C: draw something real, something pretend, and something that could be changed, then name a grown-up to ask. Pairs share one drawing with each other using the frame "This is ___ because ___." Invite two pairs to share with the class.

    Facilitator noteListen for reasons, not just labels. "It's changed because fish can't skateboard and it looks like a photo" is strong evidence.

Paper or screen

Unplugged

The entire lesson works with the printed strips, your voice, and the paper cut-out demonstration. The paper "change a picture" move in the Explore step shows children how editing works without a screen.

Digital

In the Explore step, project a real photo you took of your classroom and let children name what they recognize. Then, as the class watches, type into a district-approved AI image generator: "A realistic photo of a dog as tall as a house, standing next to a car." Show the result beside your real photo and ask: "Which one is real? How can we tell? Could we tell if we didn't know?" Children never use the image tool themselves; you drive it on the projector.

Does it need a screen? The sort and poses work best unplugged. The projected demo earns its few minutes: seeing a computer make a realistic picture from words, right beside a real classroom photo, shows children why "it has a picture" is not proof, which a description alone cannot do.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Children give a clue for their choice ("lions don't live in kitchens," "it's a storybook"), not just a label.
  • Children tell pretend apart from changed by asking whether it is trying to look real.
  • During the demo or reflection, children say that a picture can look real and still be made by a computer, and name a grown-up to ask.

Adaptations

Kindergarten
Start with only REAL and PRETEND for the first six strips, then add CHANGED with the paper cut-out demo.
Grade 2
Pairs write a short "changed" caption for a real classroom photo (for example, "Our class pet is a baby dragon") and explain how a reader could check it.
Emergent bilinguals
The poses carry meaning without words. Add simple sketches to the strips and teach "real," "pretend," and "changed" in both languages.
Children who can't move freely
Use three colored cards (green for real, yellow for pretend, blue for changed) instead of poses.

Standards connections

Children tell real, pretend-on-purpose, and changed or computer-made pictures apart and give a clue, early media literacy about genre and trustworthiness.

What comes next

Invite families to play "Real, Pretend, or Changed?" with pictures they see together in books, on cereal boxes, or on a screen, asking the three detective questions. Next lesson, Ask Two adds the habit of checking what a computer tells us with a second source.
