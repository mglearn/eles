Password Power & Secret Keepers

Students rank passwords from weak to strong, build silly-but-strong practice passphrases, and sort tricky moments into keep it secret, share with a trusted grown-up, or tell a grown-up now.

Overview

By third grade, many students have school logins, game accounts, or a family tablet code. This lesson gives them three habits to protect those accounts. First, teams line up password cards from weakest to strongest and discover that long, random passphrases beat short passwords full of personal details. Next, they build silly practice passphrases from word cards (never their real ones). Then they become Secret Keepers, sorting tricky moments, including a message that asks for a password and a phone call from a voice that sounds just like a relative, into keep it secret, share only with a trusted grown-up, or tell a grown-up now. They leave with a plan for what to do when an account acts weird.

Objectives

  • Students will explain what makes a password strong (long, random words, not personal information, different for each account) and rank examples.
  • Students will build a practice passphrase and explain why it is hard to guess but easy to remember.
  • Students will decide who, if anyone, can know a password in a set of scenarios, and explain why friends are not on the list.
  • Students will name the steps to take when an account acts weird, including telling a trusted grown-up, and one way to check a surprising call or message.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: Password Line-Up (1 set per team, cut apart)
  • Handout B: Secret Keeper Sort (1 set per team, cut apart, plus the three category labels)
  • Handout C: My Password Power Plan (1 per student; the last section goes home)
  • Word slips for building passphrases: write 30–40 fun, unrelated words (taco, rocket, purple, umbrella, giraffe, pickle, cloud, trumpet…) on scraps, and put them in a bag
  • Tape or a clothesline and clothespins for the password line-up (optional)

On screen

  • Optional: the teacher's device, projected, to show where a school account's password-change and sign-out settings live (use a demo or teacher account, never a student's)
  • Optional: a document camera to show the line-up

Before you start

  1. Print and cut Handouts A and B for each team. Keep the backs (the key) for yourself.
  2. Write 30–40 random fun words on slips and put them in a bag or envelope. Avoid names, places, or anything personal.
  3. Decide what your school's real rule is for who can help with a student's school password (for example, the teacher or the campus tech helper), so you can name it accurately in the Secret Keeper step.
  4. Remind yourself of the lesson's one firm rule: students never write or say their real passwords today. Everything is practice.

Step by step

  1. 10–4 min

    Hook

    The locked diary

    Hold up a notebook and say: "This is my secret diary. It has a lock. The combination is… my birthday!" Ask: "Is that a good lock?" Students will say no: lots of people know your birthday. Say: "Accounts online have locks too. They're called passwords. Today you'll learn to make powerful ones, and to be a Secret Keeper who knows who is allowed to know them."

    Facilitator noteMake the one firm rule clear right away: "We will never say or write our real passwords today. Everything we make is practice."

  2. 24–12 min

    Explore

    Password Line-Up

    Teams get the ten Handout A cards and line them up (on the table or on a clothesline) from weakest to strongest. They must agree on a reason for their top and bottom card. Then reveal the key: read the backs for a few cards and have teams adjust. Build a class list of Password Power rules on the board: long beats short; random words beat real words about you; no names, birthdays, pets, or teams; a different password for every account; never share it with friends.

    Facilitator noteTeams often rank a short password with symbols (like the "B@ddy!" card) above a long passphrase. That's the key misconception. Ask: "Which one would be harder for someone to guess if they knew a lot about you? Which one could you remember?"

  3. 312–18 min

    Create

    Build a practice passphrase

    Each student draws four word slips from the bag without looking, and writes them in the practice box on Handout C as a silly passphrase (for example, pickle-trumpet-cloud-giraffe). Then they draw a quick picture that helps them remember it. Say: "This is how you make a real one at home with a grown-up: random words, a funny picture in your head. But this one is only for practice. Real passwords don't go on paper at school."

    Facilitator notePoint out why drawing without looking matters: words you choose yourself tend to be things you like, and people who know you might guess those. Random picks are harder to guess.

  4. 418–28 min

    Practice

    Secret Keeper Sort

    Teams sort the twelve Handout B scenarios into Keep it secret, Share only with a trusted grown-up, and Tell a grown-up now. Circulate and ask, "Why?" Then discuss the tricky ones as a class. For the two-step check card, explain: "Some accounts send a second check, like a code to a grown-up's phone, when someone signs in. It's like a second lock. If a code shows up and you didn't try to sign in, that's a signal to tell a grown-up." For the voice card, say: "Computer tools can now copy a person's voice. A family can pick a secret safe word to check that a caller is really who they say they are."

    Facilitator noteKeep the voice-copying piece calm and brief. The takeaway isn't fear; it's a family habit: hang up and check another way, or ask the safe word. Name your school's real rule for who helps with school passwords.

  5. 528–35 min

    Apply

    When an account acts weird

    Read this aloud: "Jada logs into her game account and her avatar has new clothes she didn't buy, and her friends say she sent them strange messages." Ask teams: "What should Jada do first? Next?" Build the steps together: 1. Stop and don't click anything new. 2. Tell a trusted grown-up right away. 3. With the grown-up, change the password to a new, strong one. 4. Turn on a two-step check if the account has one. 5. Tell friends not to open the strange messages. Students copy the steps they'd use into Handout C.

    Facilitator noteEmphasize that Jada is not in trouble. Kids hide account problems when they fear punishment. Say: "Telling a grown-up fast is the smart move, every time."

  6. 635–40 min

    Reflect

    Secret Keeper pledge

    Students finish Handout C on their own: who their trusted grown-ups are for passwords, and one Password Power rule they will use this week. Collect or check it as evidence. Close by reading the family section aloud: "Tonight, talk with your family about making a safe word. Decide it together, and don't write it down on this paper."

    Facilitator noteStudents without a family phone or accounts can still do the plan: school accounts count, and a safe word works for any family.

Paper or screen

Unplugged

Everything runs on paper: the line-up cards, the word-slip bag, the sort, and the plan. The line-up works well as a movement activity: give ten students one card each and have the class arrange them across the front of the room from weakest to strongest.

Digital

On the teacher's device, projected, show where a demo or teacher account keeps its password-change, sign-out-everywhere, and two-step settings, so students can recognize those words later. Never display a student's account. Students do not need devices or accounts for this lesson. For extra practice, share the Secret Keeper scenarios as a teacher-led slide poll where students vote with hand signals.

Does it need a screen? This lesson is better unplugged: the habits are decisions and conversations, and practicing them on paper keeps real passwords off screens and out of sight. A short projected tour of real account settings earns its time by making "change your password" and "two-step check" concrete.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Teams rank the long random passphrase above short passwords with symbols and give a reason about length or guessability.
  • Students sort "a best friend asks for your password" into Keep it secret and explain that friends don't need passwords, even kind ones.
  • Students list "tell a trusted grown-up" as an early step when an account acts weird, without suggesting they would hide it.
  • Students name one way to check a surprising call or message (hang up and call back on a known number, ask the safe word, ask a grown-up).

Adaptations

Grade 3
Use six line-up cards (1, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10) and the sort with two categories: Keep it secret and Tell a grown-up. Draw three word slips instead of four.
Grade 5
Add a discussion of why using the same password everywhere is risky: if one account is broken into, the others are open too. Students explain it to a partner with the diary-and-house-key comparison.
Emergent bilinguals
Allow word slips in home languages (a mix of languages makes a strong passphrase). Pre-teach password, secret, trusted, and code with gestures and pictures.
Students with reading needs
Read sort cards aloud in teams and let students sort by pointing or placing. Provide a picture icon for each category (a lock, a grown-up, a megaphone).

Reflect

  • Who are my trusted grown-ups for passwords, and why isn't my best friend on the list?
  • What will I do if something about an account, a message, or a call feels weird?

What comes next

At home, students use the last section of Handout C to talk with their family about choosing a family safe word and about who helps them with passwords, and they teach a family member the passphrase trick. In a later lesson, connect to Phish Tank IRL to practice spotting scam messages.
