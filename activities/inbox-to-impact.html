Inbox to Impact: Professional Digital Communication

Staff revise five realistic messages to families and colleagues for clarity, kindness, privacy, accessibility, and translation, and learn to review an AI-drafted message before it goes out.

Overview

A single email can build trust with a family or undo a month of it. In this session, participants work as editors on five fictional messages: a sharp reply to a parent, a staff email that exposes student information, a jargon-heavy newsletter for a multilingual community, an AI-drafted message with a made-up policy in it, and a video-meeting invite missing everything people need. Using a shared Before You Hit Send checklist, tables revise each message, then write one real message of their own to use this week. The big idea: professional communication is relationship work, and privacy, access, and AI review are part of being kind.

Objectives

  • Participants will revise at least three mock messages for a clear purpose, a warm and professional tone, and plain language.
  • Participants will identify information that should never go in an email or group message and choose a safer channel for it.
  • Participants will plan how a message reaches multilingual families accurately, including when machine translation needs a fluent human check.
  • Participants will review an AI-drafted message for accuracy, tone, and privacy, and name what they changed before sending.

Materials

On paper

  • Handout A: Five Messages That Need a Second Look (1 per table)
  • Handout B: Before You Hit Send checklist (1 per person; many keep it by their desk)
  • Handout C: Message Makeover Log (1 per table)
  • Colored pens for editing, sticky notes, chart paper titled "Our Team's Message Norms"

On screen

  • Optional: an email app or shared document to draft the final message
  • Optional: a generative AI chatbot your district approves, projected by the facilitator, to generate a first draft live for the group to review
  • Optional: your district's approved translation service or tool, for the translation discussion

Before you start

  1. Print Handout A for each table and Handout B for each person.
  2. Find out your district's rules and approved routes for three things: sharing student information, translation and interpretation for families, and using AI tools for staff communication. Have them ready to name.
  3. Know who in your district handles translation requests and how long they take, so participants leave with a real route, not just advice.
  4. If you'll demo an AI draft live, test your prompt first and never include real names or student details in it.

Step by step

  1. 10–5 min

    Hook

    Read it as the family would

    Read Message 1 from Handout A aloud in a neutral voice. Then ask participants to imagine they are Ms. Delgado, a parent reading it on her phone at 9 p.m. after a long shift. Ask: "How does she feel? What does she do next?" Take a few answers. Say: "The words we send are the only part of us people see. Today we'll edit five messages and leave with one of our own ready to send."

    Facilitator noteParticipants often laugh in recognition. Honor it: everyone has sent a message they wish they could revise.

  2. 25–12 min

    Model

    Think aloud with the checklist

    Project Handout B and model it on Message 1. "Purpose: what do I want her to know and do? That should be in the first two lines. Tone: I'll start with something true and positive about her child, describe the issue as behavior and facts, not labels, and offer a next step together. Privacy: this is about her child only, so it's fine to send to her, but not to copy the whole grade-level team. Access: short sentences, no acronyms, and I'll check which language she prefers. Channel: some of this might be better as a phone call." Rewrite the opening lines together on chart paper.

    Facilitator noteName the channel choice explicitly. Some messages shouldn't be emails at all.

  3. 312–30 min

    Practice

    Editing stations

    Tables work through Messages 2–5 on Handout A, about four minutes each, logging each on Handout C: What's wrong, Who might be hurt or left out, The fix, and Better channel? For Message 4 (the AI draft), tables must underline every factual claim and mark it Verified, Wrong, or Can't tell before editing the tone. Tables rewrite at least the first three lines of each message in colored pen.

    Facilitator noteMessage 2 surprises people. Listen for the realization that a helpful reminder to staff exposed health and discipline information to a whole list. Message 4 usually reveals that the AI invented a policy and a date.

  4. 430–40 min

    Debrief

    What never goes in an email?

    Pull the room together. Build a quick list on chart paper: Never in an email or group message: student health or disability information, discipline details, grades or test scores of any student other than the family's own child, student ID numbers, passwords or login codes, home addresses, custody or legal matters, and anything you'd be uncomfortable seeing forwarded. Then discuss two questions: "When does a message need a human translator, not just a tool?" and "What's your personal rule for reviewing an AI draft before you send it?"

    Facilitator noteKeep legal specifics tied to your district policy, and point people to whoever handles student records questions. The goal is habits, not legal advice.

  5. 540–46 min

    Apply

    Video-meeting norms in two minutes

    Using Message 5, tables list what a good virtual meeting invite and meeting include. Collect on the norms chart: a clear purpose and agenda, the date with time zone and length, one link and a phone option, an interpreter arranged in advance when needed, captions turned on, camera optional, a note on whether it will be recorded and why, and no student information shown on a shared screen. Ask: "Who might be left out of a video meeting, and how do we plan for them?"

    Facilitator noteFamilies without reliable internet, those working shifts, and those who need interpretation are the usual answers. A phone option and a written summary help.

  6. 646–56 min

    Create

    One real message

    Each person drafts one message they actually need to send in the next week, to a family or a colleague, on the back of Handout C or in an email app. Use fictional placeholders only, no real student names. Swap with a partner, who reviews it with Handout B and gives one specific suggestion. If anyone used AI for the first draft, the partner asks: "What did you check and change?"

    Facilitator noteThis is the evidence of learning: a revised, ready-to-send message and the reason for each change.

  7. 756–60 min

    Reflect

    Our team's message norms

    Each table adds one norm to the chart titled "Our Team's Message Norms," such as "Purpose in the first two lines" or "We read AI drafts out loud before sending." Leaders commit to one norm they'll model in their own messages this month.

    Facilitator noteLeaders: photograph the chart and share it with staff as a starting draft for campus communication norms.

Paper or screen

Unplugged

This session is designed to run entirely on paper. Tables edit printed messages in colored pen, log changes on Handout C, and build norms on chart paper. For the AI step, Message 4 is a printed AI draft, so no tool is needed to practice the review habit.

Digital

Share Handout A as a document so tables can use suggestion mode to edit, and draft the final message in an email app, saved as a draft, not sent. The facilitator can project an approved AI chatbot to generate a first draft from a prompt with no real names, then have the room review it with Handout B. In a virtual session, run editing stations in breakout rooms and apply the video-meeting norms to the session itself.

Does it need a screen? Paper is the better default here: editing a printed message slows people down enough to notice tone, privacy, and access problems. A live AI draft adds value only if the group reviews it; watching it appear fluent and still wrong makes the case for a human check before sending.

Evidence of learning

What you should be able to see or collect if it worked.

  • Revised messages state the purpose and any action needed in the first two lines, in plain language.
  • Tables identify every privacy problem in Message 2 and name a safer channel for each piece of information.
  • For the AI draft, tables mark individual claims as verified, wrong, or can't tell, and correct the invented policy before editing tone.
  • Each participant leaves with one revised real message and can explain at least one change they made and why.

Adaptations

Front office and support staff
Swap Message 3 for a phone-message follow-up or a school closure text, and add a station on what to say when a family asks for information you can't share.
Leaders
Add a sixth message: a campus-wide announcement about a change. Run it through the five-question check, with extra time on "How will families understand the change?"
Multilingual campuses
Invite a staff member who serves as an interpreter to co-facilitate the translation discussion and share what makes a message easier to translate well.
Short on time
Run Messages 1, 2, and 4 only (30 minutes), and send Handout B home as the takeaway.

Standards connections

Staff revise family and colleague messages for tone, privacy, translation, and AI accuracy, modeling the respectful digital communication students are taught in digital citizenship.
