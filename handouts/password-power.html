Password Power & Secret Keepers

Students rank passwords from weak to strong, build silly-but-strong practice passphrases, and sort tricky moments into keep it secret, share with a trusted grown-up, or tell a grown-up now.

By third grade, many students have school logins, game accounts, or a family tablet code. This lesson gives them three habits to protect those accounts. First, teams line up password cards from weakest to strongest and discover that long, random passphrases beat short passwords full of personal details. Next, they build silly practice passphrases from word cards (never their real ones). Then they become Secret Keepers, sorting tricky moments, including a message that asks for a password and a phone call from a voice that sounds just like a relative, into keep it secret, share only with a trusted grown-up, or tell a grown-up now. They leave with a plan for what to do when an account acts weird.

Still weak. Swapping a letter for a symbol is a trick people know about, and it's short.

Weak. Names and birthdays are personal information people can find.

Weak. One common word, easy to guess.
Card 8
Strong. Four random words: long, hard to guess, and easy to remember with a silly picture.
Card 9
Strongest in this set. Five random words. Long beats short.
Card 10
Tricky! A strong password turns weak when it's reused. If one account gets broken into, the others are open too. Every account needs its own.

Handout B: Secret Keeper Sort

ItemSortWhy
Your best friend says, "Tell me your game password so I can help you beat the next level."Keep it secretFriends, even kind ones, don't need your password. Friendships change, and it's your account.
Your mom or dad needs your password to help set up the family tablet.Share only with a trusted grown-upThe grown-ups who take care of you and your accounts can know it.
A pop-up in a game says, "You won 10,000 free coins! Type your password to claim them."Tell a grown-up nowA real game never needs your password to give you a prize. This is a trick to steal your account.
A classmate is standing behind you watching while you type your school password.Keep it secretCover the keyboard or ask them to step back, kindly. Passwords stay private.
You get a message: "This is the Game Help Team. Your account will be deleted today unless you send your password."Tell a grown-up nowRushing you and asking for a password are scam signals. Real help teams don't ask for passwords.
Your teacher helps you reset your school login after you forgot it.Share only with a trusted grown-upThe grown-ups in charge of school accounts can help, following your school's rules.
A code shows up on your grandma's phone that says "Your sign-in code is 482913," but nobody in your family was trying to sign in.Tell a grown-up nowThat second check means someone may be trying to get into the account. Never share the code; tell a grown-up.
Someone calls. The voice sounds exactly like your uncle, and he says he's in trouble and needs your family's bank code right now.Tell a grown-up nowComputer tools can copy voices. Hang up and have a grown-up call your uncle back on the number they already have, or ask for the family safe word.
A new friend in an online game asks what your password hint question is ("What's your pet's name?").Keep it secretHint answers can unlock accounts. They're secrets too.
You see your older cousin's password on a sticky note on the fridge.Keep it secretDon't use it or share it. You might let your cousin know it's not a safe spot.
Your game account suddenly shows items you didn't buy and messages you didn't send.Tell a grown-up nowSomeone else may be using your account. Tell a grown-up fast so you can change the password together.
Your guardian wants to turn on a two-step check for your account so a code goes to their phone.Share only with a trusted grown-upA second lock managed by a trusted grown-up makes your account much safer.
