Minutes With a Purpose: A Screen Time Audit

Teams audit a fictional district's school-device minutes by purpose, not just by total, and draft grade-band guidance that protects unplugged time and asks for evidence of learning.

Texas HB 1481 (2025) settled the personal-phone question for the school day, and it explicitly leaves out devices the district provides. How much and how well students use school-issued devices is still your district's call. In this audit, teams read a fictional district's device-use snapshot critically, sort real-feeling classroom uses by purpose (creating, researching and collaborating, practicing with feedback, or just watching), and apply the 2026 ELE question: "What evidence shows the technology improved the learning?" They leave with draft grade-band guidance and an honest, privacy-safe plan to gather their own local data. The goal is intentional, age-appropriate use judged by learning, not a blanket cut.

TCEA ELE indicators

  • L3.2 I can interpret complex digital data sets and communicate findings effectively using data visualization tools.
  • L4.3 I am aware of strategies to address digital divides and promote responsible technology use.
  • L3.1 I know how to use digital data tools to drive school improvement efforts.
  • L1.1 I know how to create and communicate a compelling vision for technology integration in the school.
  • C3.1 I know how to use data to inform instructional decisions.

Purpose Sort: cards to sort

Cut apart the strips. Sort each one onto the mat and be ready to explain why.

Sheet 1 of 2

1. Kindergarten: the class watches a 4-minute read-aloud video of a picture book while the teacher sets up centers. No discussion follows.

2. Grade 4: pairs build a stop-motion clay video explaining the water cycle, then revise one scene after peer feedback.

3. Grade 7 science: pairs find three sources on whether a local creek is safe for swimming and record who published each one.

4. Grade 3: 20 minutes a day on an adaptive math app. The teacher reads the weekly report but never changes groups or reteaches from it.

5. Grade 10: students watch a 12-minute documentary segment, pausing at three teacher-set stops to write a claim and the evidence for it.

6. Grades 9–12: students take a 20-question online benchmark test.

7. Grade 6: students co-write a shared document with a class in another Texas city comparing a month of local rainfall records.

8. Grade 8: students who finish early are told to "get on your device" and quietly watch short videos until the bell.

9. Grade 1: children use a drawing app to make a labeled picture of a plant, then tell the teacher what each label means.

10. Grade 11: students type an argumentative essay, the teacher leaves comments in the document, and students revise twice.

11. Grade 5: 10 minutes of typing practice that shows speed and accuracy after each round.

12. Grade 12: a student messages a teacher through the learning platform to ask a question about a lab procedure.

1. Kindergarten: the class watches a 4-minute read-aloud video of a picture book while the teacher sets up centers. No discussion follows.Consume / watchNo task is attached and no evidence is left. A live read-aloud with talk would teach more; the video could still work with a question before and after.
2. Grade 4: pairs build a stop-motion clay video explaining the water cycle, then revise one scene after peer feedback.CreateStudents make an original explanation and revise it. The video and the revision are the evidence.
3. Grade 7 science: pairs find three sources on whether a local creek is safe for swimming and record who published each one.Research & collaborateThe evidence is the source log and the reasoning about who is behind each source.
4. Grade 3: 20 minutes a day on an adaptive math app. The teacher reads the weekly report but never changes groups or reteaches from it.Practice with feedback & assessStudents get feedback, but the loop stops there. Close call: it becomes strong practice only when an adult acts on the data.
5. Grade 10: students watch a 12-minute documentary segment, pausing at three teacher-set stops to write a claim and the evidence for it.Research & collaborateThe report calls this 'watch' because it's a video site, but students are gathering and evaluating evidence. The claims on paper are the evidence.
6. Grades 9–12: students take a 20-question online benchmark test.Practice with feedback & assessAssessment. Ask how fast results come back and whether anyone uses them to change instruction.
7. Grade 6: students co-write a shared document with a class in another Texas city comparing a month of local rainfall records.Research & collaborateThe device makes the partnership possible; the evidence is the comparison and the questions students ask each other.
8. Grade 8: students who finish early are told to "get on your device" and quietly watch short videos until the bell.Consume / watchFiller time. It shows up as school-device minutes with no learning purpose. An unplugged extension or a reading choice would do more.
9. Grade 1: children use a drawing app to make a labeled picture of a plant, then tell the teacher what each label means.CreateThe telling is the evidence. Crayons would work too; the app helps only if it lets children record or revise their labels.
10. Grade 11: students type an argumentative essay, the teacher leaves comments in the document, and students revise twice.CreateWriting and revising is making. Close call with feedback: the visible revision history is strong evidence of learning.
11. Grade 5: 10 minutes of typing practice that shows speed and accuracy after each round.Practice with feedback & assessImmediate feedback on a skill students will use. Keep it short and purposeful.
12. Grade 12: a student messages a teacher through the learning platform to ask a question about a lab procedure.Research & collaborateCommunication in service of learning. Few minutes, high value; minute counts undervalue it.
13. Grade 6: a points-and-badges review game where the fastest answer wins, and no one explains answers.Practice with feedback & assessIt is practice with right/wrong feedback, but speed can reward guessing. Close call: add a pause to explain one answer and the evidence improves.
14. Grade 7: students scroll a recommended-video feed "for research" with no question to answer and nothing to turn in.Consume / watchWithout a question, the feed chooses the path. Give students a question and a source log and it becomes research.
