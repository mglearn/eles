From Values to Guidance: Drafting a Campus AI Statement

Leadership teams start with what they value, sort draft clauses, and write a one-page generative AI guidance statement that people can actually follow.

Many campuses have AI guidance that is either a ban nobody enforces or a list of tools nobody reads. This session builds guidance the other way around: teams choose the values that matter most for learning on their campus, sort a deck of draft clauses to find which ones are clear, fair, and enforceable, and then draft a one-page statement covering acceptable use, disclosure, privacy, and equity. Along the way they critique an AI-generated draft, which models the same judgment the guidance asks of students and staff.

In this packet

  • Handout A: Values Cards (cut-apart cards)
  • Handout B: Clause Sort (card sort)
  • Handout C: Guidance Statement Drafting Frame (worksheet)
  • Facilitator key (last page)

TCEA ELE indicators

  • L1.1 I know how to create and communicate a compelling vision for technology integration in the school.
  • AI2.1 I know how to apply ethical principles when using or developing AI for education.
  • AI2.3 I am aware of the importance of transparency and accountability in AI systems.
  • L4.1 I know how to create a positive and inclusive digital learning climate.

Handout B for From Values to Guidance: Drafting a Campus AI Statement

Clause Sort: cards to sort

Cut apart the strips. Sort each one onto the mat and be ready to explain why.

Sheet 1 of 2

Students may use approved AI tools to brainstorm, get feedback, or practice, unless a teacher states otherwise for a specific assignment.

AI may not be used for schoolwork.

Each assignment will state its AI expectation: no AI, AI for specific steps, or AI allowed with disclosure.

AI tools may inform, but may not decide, grades, discipline, placement, or academic integrity findings.

When AI helped with your work, add a short note saying which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed.

Students must be honest about AI use.

Staff who use AI to draft family communications, feedback, or reports will review every word and remain responsible for the content.

AI detection software will be used to identify cheating.

Do not enter student names, grades, health information, or plan details into any AI tool that is not on the district-approved list.

Be careful with personal data.

Tools are approved only after the district confirms a data privacy agreement covering how student data is stored, used, and deleted.

Creating or sharing AI-generated images, audio, or video of a real student or staff member without consent is prohibited and will be handled under the student code of conduct.

Handout B: Clause Sort

ItemSortWhy
Students may use approved AI tools to brainstorm, get feedback, or practice, unless a teacher states otherwise for a specific assignment.Acceptable UseClear default plus teacher discretion. A strong clause.
AI may not be used for schoolwork.Acceptable UseWeak: unenforceable, and it blocks accommodations and legitimate uses like text-to-speech.
Each assignment will state its AI expectation: no AI, AI for specific steps, or AI allowed with disclosure.Acceptable UseStrong: moves the decision to the task, where it belongs.
AI tools may inform, but may not decide, grades, discipline, placement, or academic integrity findings.Acceptable UseStrong: keeps human judgment in high-stakes decisions.
When AI helped with your work, add a short note saying which tool you used, what you asked it, and what you changed.DisclosureStrong: specific and teachable; it also produces evidence of the student's thinking.
Students must be honest about AI use.DisclosureWeak: a value, not guidance. It doesn't say what honest disclosure looks like.
Staff who use AI to draft family communications, feedback, or reports will review every word and remain responsible for the content.DisclosureStrong: holds adults to the same standard as students.
AI detection software will be used to identify cheating.DisclosureWeak and risky: detectors can falsely flag students, including multilingual writers. Use conversation and process evidence instead.
Do not enter student names, grades, health information, or plan details into any AI tool that is not on the district-approved list.PrivacyStrong: specific data types and a clear boundary.
Be careful with personal data.PrivacyWeak: no one can act on it. Which data? Careful how?
Tools are approved only after the district confirms a data privacy agreement covering how student data is stored, used, and deleted.PrivacyStrong: ties approval to FERPA and COPPA obligations and a real review process.
Creating or sharing AI-generated images, audio, or video of a real student or staff member without consent is prohibited and will be handled under the student code of conduct.PrivacyStrong: addresses synthetic media harms directly and connects to existing consequences.
No assignment will require a paid AI tool or a personal account.Equity and AccessStrong: removes a cost barrier families shouldn't carry.
Students who need AI-based supports as accommodations (such as text-to-speech or speech-to-text) may always use them.Equity and AccessStrong: protects accommodations from blanket restrictions.
Students without home internet should find a way to complete AI-assisted work.Equity and AccessWeak and inequitable: puts the burden on the student. Offer in-school time or non-AI alternatives.
Families will receive this guidance in the languages spoken at home, with a chance to ask questions at a family event.Equity and AccessStrong: answers "How will families understand the change?"
