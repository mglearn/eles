Remix Rights

Students sort real-world remix decisions using copyright, Creative Commons, and fair use, then write credit lines that include honest notes about AI help.

"It was on the internet, so it's free" is one of the most common student beliefs, and it's wrong. Almost everything people create is protected by copyright automatically, but creators can choose to share through Creative Commons licenses, and fair use allows some limited uses for things like commentary, criticism, and teaching. In this activity, students sort fourteen remix decisions, learn what the Creative Commons license symbols mean, and write credit lines for a project, including an honest note when AI helped. The goal is not fear of using anything; it's knowing how to remix responsibly.

  • AI2.3 I am aware of the importance of transparency and accountability in AI systems.
  • S1.1 I know how to use technology to support my learning.
  • S2.3 I am aware of the potential risks of sharing content online.

Handout A: Remix Decisions Sort

ItemSortWhy
1 · Using a CC BY photo of a canyon in your class slideshow, with a credit line.Go ahead, with creditCC BY allows any reuse as long as you credit the creator.
2 · Using a CC BY-NC song in a video you plan to sell online.Get permission or choose something elseNC means noncommercial only. Selling is commercial.
3 · Quoting two sentences from a novel in your book review for English class, with the page number.Might be fair use: explainCommentary (why), a small amount (how much), and it won't replace the book (market).
4 · Copying a whole article from a news site onto your class blog.Get permission or choose something elseThe whole work, reposted publicly, could replace the original. Link to it and summarize instead.
5 · Using a CC0 illustration of a sunflower on your science poster.Go ahead, with creditCC0 requires no credit, but giving it anyway is good practice.
6 · Showing a 10-second clip from a movie in a class presentation analyzing its camera angles.Might be fair use: explainTeaching and criticism (why), a short clip (how much).
7 · Taking a classmate's drawing from the shared class folder and using it as your profile picture.Get permission or choose something elseYour classmate owns their art. Ask first.
8 · Adding text to a CC BY-SA photo for a poster and sharing the poster under CC BY-SA.Go ahead, with creditBY-SA allows changes if you credit and share your version under the same license.
9 · Adding captions to a CC BY-ND photo and posting the edited version.Get permission or choose something elseND means no derivatives: you may share it only unchanged.
10 · Using a popular band's entire hit song as background music for a school video posted publicly.Get permission or choose something elseThe whole song, used for background, not commentary. Choose openly licensed music.
11 · Using an AI tool your teacher approved to make a poster background, then writing "All artwork by me."Go ahead, with creditUsing it is fine if class rules allow it, but be honest: add a note that says what AI made and what you made.
12 · Using a photo from image search results that shows no license information.Get permission or choose something elseNo license shown means assume all rights reserved. Use a license filter or an openly licensed collection.
13 · Making a short parody of a TV cartoon's theme song for a class project that pokes fun at the show.Might be fair use: explainParody that comments on the original (why) can be fair use, especially if it borrows only what it needs.
14 · Posting an AI response that repeats several pages of a copyrighted novel word for word.Get permission or choose something elseCopying long passages is still copying, even if an AI produced it.

Handout B: License Cards

Creative Commons
Share, remix, even sell, as long as you credit the creator.
Creative Commons
Share and remix with credit, and share your new version under the same license.
Creative Commons
Share and remix with credit, but not to make money.
Creative Commons
Share it with credit, but only exactly as it is. Don't share changed versions.
Creative Commons
Remix with credit, not for money, and share your version under the same license.
Creative Commons
The most limited CC license: share unchanged, with credit, not for money.
Creative Commons
The creator gave up their rights as far as the law allows. Use it freely; credit is optional but kind.
No restrictions
No copyright applies, often because it expired long ago. Use it freely, and credit it anyway so others can find the source.
Default
The creator keeps all rights. Ask permission, unless your use might be fair use.
