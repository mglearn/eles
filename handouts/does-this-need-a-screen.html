Does This Need a Screen?

A fast, argument-starting card sort where teachers decide which classroom tasks are best unplugged, which earn their screen time, and where AI adds value, and have to say why.

The screen-time debate usually gets stuck at "more" or "less." This quick sort replaces that with a better question: for this task and these students, what does a screen add to the evidence of learning, and what does it cost in attention, connection, and wellbeing? Groups sort 14 real-feeling classroom tasks into four piles, argue the close calls, and leave with a five-question check they can use while planning tomorrow. It works well as a staff-meeting opener or the first 20 minutes of a longer session.

Five-Question Screen Check

NameDate

Run one task from this week's plans through these questions before deciding.

YesNot sureNo
I can name the evidence of learning this task should produce.
The screen (or AI tool) makes that evidence easier to produce, see, or give feedback on.
The same evidence would be weaker or slower to get without the screen.
I've considered what the screen costs here: attention, face-to-face talk, movement, or emotional connection.
If AI is involved, students must question, verify, or decide something about its output, not just accept it.
Students' personal information is protected in any tool this task uses.

Answer key and notes

Handout A: Screen or No Screen? Sort

ItemSortWhy
Kindergartners practice forming the letters in their names.Unplugged is bestLetter formation is a fine-motor skill; pencils, crayons, and tracing in sand build it better than tapping a screen.
A morning circle where students share how they're feeling today.Unplugged is bestEye contact, tone, and being heard in person are the point. A screen would thin out the connection.
A 3rd-grade class plays a paper "guess the next word" game to learn how a chatbot writes answers.Unplugged is bestHandling the cards makes an invisible process visible; the concept, not the tool, is what's being learned.
High school students write the first draft of a personal narrative about a moment that changed them.Unplugged is bestThe drafting is the learning. An AI draft would do the remembering, choosing, and shaping for them. (Typing on a device is fine; AI generation is not.)
Two teams rehearse a formal debate before a class tournament.Unplugged is bestListening, rebuttal, and reading the room are live, face-to-face skills.
4th graders compare a month of daily temperatures from two cities and explain the difference.Digital earns its placeA spreadsheet graphs 60 numbers in seconds, so class time goes to interpreting the pattern instead of plotting points.
A middle school class interviews a partner class in another state about local landforms.Digital earns its placeA video call is the only practical way to talk live with that class. The screen creates the learning opportunity.
A student whose handwriting is slow and painful because of a motor disability writes a story.Digital earns its placeTyping or speech-to-text removes a barrier so the evidence shows the student's storytelling, not their handwriting. (Follow the student's plan.)
8th graders practice solving two-step equations and need to know right away whether each answer is correct.Digital earns its placeImmediate feedback on each problem helps students catch errors before they practice them 20 times. A worked-out answer key can also do this.
11th graders strengthen an argumentative essay by answering the strongest counterarguments to their claim.AI adds value (with guardrails)An approved chatbot can act as a sparring partner that generates counterarguments. Students must verify and rebut them, and disclose the use.
A teacher needs a science article rewritten at two reading levels for newcomers learning English.AI adds value (with guardrails)AI can produce leveled drafts quickly. The teacher must check every fact and term against the original before students see it.
6th graders record a grandparent's memories for a family oral-history project.It depends: say on whatDepends on purpose and consent. Recording preserves the voice, but the interview itself is human; notes on paper may be enough.
7th graders build a model of an animal cell.It depends: say on whatDepends on the goal. A clay model shows structure you can hold; a simulation shows processes over time.
Before a sensitive discussion, the teacher wants every student's honest opinion, anonymously.It depends: say on whatAn anonymous digital poll or folded paper slips can both work. Choose based on class size, trust, and whether results need to be shown instantly.
