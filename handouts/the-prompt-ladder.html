The Prompt Ladder

Teachers climb eight rungs of prompts for the same classroom task, predict each mock AI output before reading it, and weigh what every added detail gains and what it costs.

A chatbot predicts its answer from patterns it learned plus the words you give it, so your prompt is the context it predicts from. Adding detail usually narrows the output toward what you want, but not always for free. In this unplugged session, teams climb a "ladder" of eight prompts for one real teacher task (a 4th-grade water-cycle exit ticket). At each rung, they predict what the AI will produce, read a printed mock output, and score the tradeoff. Two rungs are traps: one where a prompt's built-in misconception gets repeated back as fact, and one that works well but pastes private student information into the tool. Teachers leave with a prompting habit they can model for students: add the context that serves the learning, and stop before it costs accuracy or privacy.

Handout A for The Prompt Ladder

Prompt Rung Cards

Stack in order, face down. Flip one rung at a time, predict the output, then read it on Handout B. Card backs are the facilitator key: print them separately. Student names are fictional.

Rung 1 · Vague

"water cycle questions"

Rung 2 · Task + audience

"Write a 5-question exit ticket on the water cycle for 4th graders."

Rung 3 · + Evidence of learning

Rung 2, plus: "I want to see whether students can explain how water moves between stages, not just name the stages. At least two questions should ask students to explain why."

Rung 4 · + Learners

Rung 3, plus: "Many of my students are learning English. Use short sentences, bold the key vocabulary, and include one question students can answer with a labeled drawing."

Rung 5 · Trap: steering

"Write 5 exit-ticket questions for 4th graders that check whether they understand that clouds are made of water vapor."

Rung 6 · Trap: over-sharing

"These students failed the last quiz: Maria G. (IEP, reading accommodations) and Devon T. (504 plan, ADHD). Make a personalized version of the exit ticket for each of them."

Rung 7 · Example

Rung 4, plus: "Here is a question I like: 'Why does a puddle get smaller on a sunny day? Use the word evaporation.' Write four more like it."

Rung 8 · Follow-up

A second message after Rung 4's output: "Question 3 is too easy. Change it so students have to compare two stages of the water cycle."

Facilitator only for The Prompt Ladder

Answer key and notes

Handout A: Prompt Rung Cards

Rung 1 · Vague
The model must guess grade, number, format, and purpose, so it produces the most common pattern: generic recall at mixed levels. Cost: little effort now, lots of editing later.
Rung 2 · Task + audience
Big jump for a few words: the right length and level. Still mostly recall, because the prompt didn't say what kind of thinking to show.
Rung 3 · + Evidence of learning
Usually the best value on the ladder: stating the evidence you want changes the kind of questions, not just their wording. Evidence Before Tools, in prompt form.
Rung 4 · + Learners
Describing needs, not individuals, adds access with no privacy cost. Tradeoff: the teacher still has to check that simplified wording didn't change the science.
Rung 5 · Trap: steering
The prompt carries a misconception: clouds are tiny liquid water droplets or ice crystals, and water vapor is an invisible gas. The output builds its key on the mistake. Specific isn't the same as correct; the model tends to follow the framing you give it.
Rung 6 · Trap: over-sharing
The output is useful, and the prompt put names, disability plans, and grades into a tool. Rewrite: "Make one version with shorter sentences and a word bank, and one with three questions instead of five, including one drawing question." Same benefit, no identifying information. Follow district guidance.
Rung 7 · Example
An example matches your style well. Tradeoff: variety drops, and the model copies your example's pattern, including any weakness (every question now uses a 'Use the word ___' frame).
Rung 8 · Follow-up
Iterating targets one flaw without starting over. Cost: time, and each new version still needs checking. Ask yourself: would writing it myself be faster now?
