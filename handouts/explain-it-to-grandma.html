Explain It to Grandma

Educators draft 90-second, jargon-free explanations of AI concepts and test them live on colleagues playing a skeptical grandparent, a second grader, or a school board trustee.

Families, students, and board members are forming opinions about AI in schools right now, often from headlines. The educators who can explain it plainly and honestly, without hype or fear, shape that conversation. In this session participants pair an audience card with a concept card, draft a 90-second explanation, and then test it on a colleague who plays that audience and pushes back with the audience's real questions. Explanations get scored for plain language and for accuracy, because simple is only useful if it's also true.

In this packet

  • Handout A: Audience and Concept Cards (cut-apart cards)
  • Handout B: 90-Second Explanation Planner (worksheet)
  • Handout C: Plain and Accurate Rubric (rubric)
  • Facilitator key (last page)

TCEA ELE indicators

  • AI1.2 I can explain AI concepts in simple terms to various stakeholders.
  • AI1.1 I know how AI systems work and their capabilities in educational contexts.
  • AI1.3 I am aware of the current limitations and potential biases in AI systems.

A1 · Audience

GRANDPARENT, 74. Raised three kids, uses a smartphone for photos and video calls. Pushback: "So is it a robot? Does it know things like a person does?" and "Why would you let a child use something that makes things up?"

A2 · Audience

SECOND GRADER, 7. Loves dinosaurs, asks "why" five times in a row. Pushback: "Is it alive?" "Does it have feelings?" "Why did it say a T. rex had feathers AND didn't?"

A3 · Audience

SCHOOL BOARD TRUSTEE. Busy, practical, answers to voters. Pushback: "What problem does this solve for our students?" and "What happens when it goes wrong, and who is responsible?"

A4 · Audience

HIGH SCHOOL SENIOR. Uses AI apps daily, thinks they know more than you. Pushback: "Everybody uses it. Why does it matter how it works?" and "If it's usually right, why check?"

A5 · Audience

LOCAL NEWS REPORTER. Looking for a clear quote. Pushback: "In one sentence, is AI good or bad for kids?" and "Are teachers using it to grade?"

A6 · Audience

PARENT WORRIED ABOUT PRIVACY. Read a scary article last week. Pushback: "Where does what my kid types go?" and "Can it be used to profile my child?"

A7 · Audience

PARENT WHO SPEAKS SPANISH, WITH A SCHOOL INTERPRETER. Warm and interested, new to the district. Pushback (through the interpreter): "Will this help my child learn English, or do the work for her?"

A8 · Audience

CAMPUS STAFF MEMBER (front office). Keeps the school running and hears everything from families. Pushback: "What should I tell parents who call about AI?" and "Should I be using it for emails?"

C1 · Concept
Accuracy key: it generates text by repeatedly predicting a likely next piece of text (a token), based on patterns learned from very large amounts of training text. It doesn't look answers up the way a search engine does, unless the tool is also connected to search or documents. Not accurate: "it thinks," "it understands like a person," "it searches the internet for every answer."
C2 · Concept
Accuracy key: because it produces likely-sounding text, not verified facts. When it lacks a strong pattern for a specific fact, it can generate a plausible but false name, date, number, or citation, in the same confident tone. Remedy: verify important claims with trusted sources.
C3 · Concept
Accuracy key: models learn from human-made data, which contains gaps, stereotypes, and over- or under-representation of some groups and languages. Outputs can reflect those patterns. Bias can also come from design choices and how a tool is used. Remedy: compare outputs, question defaults, and keep humans deciding.
C4 · Concept
Accuracy key: it depends on the tool's terms and settings. Some tools store conversations or may use them to improve the product; others, often those under district agreements, limit that. Families should know which tools the district has reviewed, and students should never enter personal information into any AI tool.
C5 · Concept
Accuracy key: chatbots usually add controlled randomness when choosing each next word, so the same prompt can produce different wording or even different claims. Tools, versions, and earlier messages in the conversation also change answers. Different answers are a reason to check, not a sign someone cheated.
C6 · Concept
Accuracy key: not reliably from the text alone. AI-writing detectors are known to be unreliable, including false positives, and concerns have been raised that they flag non-native English writers unfairly. Better evidence comes from the learning process: drafts, notes, conversations, and in-class work.
C7 · Concept
Accuracy key: honest answer, no one knows the future, but AI can't do the relational, judgment-heavy parts of teaching: knowing a child, noticing when something's wrong, deciding what's worth learning. It can help with some tasks, and teachers decide where it belongs, if it belongs at all.
