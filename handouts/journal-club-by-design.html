Journal Club by Design

Coaches experience a research journal club as learners, reading a practice article with a skeptic's eye, then design their own PD series around real teacher needs and adult learning principles.

A journal club is one of the simplest ways to keep a staff connected to research, and one of the easiest to run badly: an article nobody chose, a summary nobody needed, and no change on Monday. Coaches first take part in a journal club on a fictional practice article about teaching students to pause before sharing online, practicing the same claim-checking they want teachers to teach. Then they step back, name the adult learning principles that made it work or fail, read a set of teacher needs profiles, and design a journal club series built on what their teachers actually need.

Handout A for Journal Club by Design

Practice Article: "Pause Before You Post"

This article is fictional and was written for this activity. The school, the author, the company, and all numbers are invented. Read it the way you'd want teachers to read any education article.

1Pause Before You Post: How Our Middle School Cut Misinformation Sharing in Half. By a fictional instructional coach at Juniper Ridge Middle School, Alder Springs ISD. Published in The Riverbend Educator, a fictional regional newsletter. Sponsored sidebar: "Stop misinformation before it spreads with VeriCheck AI."

2Last fall, our 7th grade team noticed students forwarding rumors in class group chats: a fake snow day, a made-up celebrity visit, a doctored photo of the cafeteria. We decided to teach one routine for six weeks: Pause, Ask, Check. Before sharing anything, students pause, ask "Who made this and why?", and check whether a reliable source reports the same thing.

3Every Monday, teachers modeled the routine for ten minutes using a real post from the week, with names removed. Students practiced in pairs and kept a small card in their planners with the three steps. Teachers reported that students began saying "pause" to each other in class, which was our favorite moment of the whole project.

4At the start and end of the six weeks, we asked students one survey question: "In the last week, how many times did you share something online before checking whether it was true?" The average answer fell from about 4 times to about 2 times. We are confident that Pause, Ask, Check cut misinformation sharing in half.

5During the same six weeks, our campus also adopted VeriCheck AI, which lets students paste a claim and receive an instant "true" or "false" rating. Many students told us they used it as their Check step. As one student put it, "If VeriCheck says it's true, I know I can share it."

6We recommend that every middle school adopt Pause, Ask, Check, ideally alongside VeriCheck AI. Our results speak for themselves.

7Questions for your journal club. What does the article claim? How do the authors know? What would you check outside the article? What's one thing worth trying, and what would you need to see to believe it worked in your classroom?

Handout B for Journal Club by Design

Principle and Teacher Profile Cards

Cut apart. The six Principle cards summarize Malcolm Knowles's assumptions about adult learners. The six Teacher Profile cards are fictional teachers at one campus.

Sheet 1 of 2

Principle · Need to know

Adults want to know why they are learning something before they invest in it.

Principle · Self-concept

Adults see themselves as responsible for their own decisions and resist being told what to do.

Principle · Experience

Adults bring rich experience that is a resource for learning and part of their identity.

Principle · Readiness

Adults are ready to learn what helps them handle real situations they face now.

Principle · Problem-centered

Adults learn best when learning is organized around problems rather than subjects.

Principle · Internal motivation

Adults respond most to internal motivators: satisfaction, growth, and seeing students succeed.

Teacher Profile · Ms. Albright, 8th grade science, 20 years

Says: "I don't need another AI session. I need time." Evidence: her students' lab reports cite sources carefully, but none of them question a source's funding or purpose.

Teacher Profile · Mr. Ngata, 6th grade ELA, first year

Says: "I want to learn every AI tool." Evidence: walkthrough notes show students copying chatbot answers into reading responses with no revision.

Handout B: Principle and Teacher Profile Cards

Principle · Need to know
Design move: open with the classroom problem the session solves, not the agenda.
Principle · Self-concept
Design move: let teachers choose articles, roles, or tracks.
Principle · Experience
Design move: ask what teachers already do before introducing anything new.
Principle · Readiness
Design move: time sessions to when the problem shows up (for example, before a research unit).
Principle · Problem-centered
Design move: frame each session as a problem of practice with a classroom try-it.
Principle · Internal motivation
Design move: bring student work back to the group so teachers see the impact.

Possible track: lateral reading about who funds science claims. Honor experience; make it short.
Teacher Profile · Mr. Ngata, 6th grade ELA, first year

Teacher Profile · Ms. Ferreira, 7th grade social studies, 8 years

Teacher Profile · Coach Dunn, PE and health, 12 years
Readiness hook: health misinformation is his problem of practice.
Teacher Profile · Mrs. Okoye, 6th grade math, 4 years
Possible digital citizenship track: app privacy, with a Family App Privacy Audit follow-up.
Teacher Profile · Mr. Ibarra, 8th grade ELA, 15 years
Need: disclosure and process evidence routines. Self-concept matters: invite him to help write the classroom norm.
