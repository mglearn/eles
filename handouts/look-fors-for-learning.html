Look-Fors That Look for Learning

Leaders sort walkthrough notes, practice on three classroom vignettes, and rebuild their technology observation tool so it captures evidence of learning instead of counting devices.

Many walkthrough forms still ask "Are students using technology?" and a yes feels like success. The updated ELEs say plainly that technology use by itself is not evidence of learning. In this session, leaders sort real-sounding walkthrough notes into evidence of learning versus evidence of device use, calibrate on three classroom vignettes (including one with generative AI), and revise a starter observation rubric so it asks what students are thinking, verifying, and deciding. They leave with a tool they can pilot in next week's walkthroughs.

In this packet

  • Handout A: Walkthrough Notes Sort (card sort)
  • Handout B: Three Classroom Vignettes (reading)
  • Handout C: Starter Look-For Rubric (rubric)
  • Facilitator key (last page)

TCEA ELE indicators

  • L2.1 I know how to support and evaluate effective technology integration practices.
  • L2.3 I am aware of research-based approaches to technology-enhanced pedagogy and their applications.
  • AI3.2 I can evaluate the effectiveness of AI-enhanced learning interventions.
  • AI4.1 I know how to promote critical thinking skills in relation to AI-generated information.

Handout B for Look-Fors That Look for Learning

Three Classroom Vignettes

These classrooms are fictional. Score each with the Starter Look-For Rubric and write the evidence beside each score.

1Vignette A · 8th Grade Science, Ms. Okada. Students are designing an investigation on what affects how fast ice melts. Ms. Okada projects a district-approved AI chatbot and asks it for ten investigation questions. She hands each team a printout of the list. Teams must choose one, reject at least two with a reason, and check whether their chosen question is testable with the materials available.

2When you visit, one team is arguing about whether "Does salt melt ice faster than sugar?" is fair because the amounts aren't specified. Another student says, "The AI said colored ice melts faster in sunlight, but it didn't say why, so we're testing it." On the board: "Reject list: what we didn't pick and why." A student who recently arrived from Brazil is working with a partner, using a bilingual glossary card.

3Vignette B · 4th Grade Reading, Mr. Hollis. No devices are in use. Students are in pairs with two short printed articles about whether the town should build a skate park, one from the town newspaper and one from a skate shop's flyer. Each pair fills in a T-chart: "What does it claim? Who made it? What do they want?"

4When you ask a student which article she trusts more, she says, "The newspaper, because the flyer is trying to sell skateboards, so of course they want the park." Her partner adds, "But the newspaper only talked to two people." Mr. Hollis circulates, asking "How do you know?" After ten minutes, pairs share one sentence each with the class.

5Vignette C · 6th Grade Math, Ms. Grant. Every student has a tablet. Students work through an adaptive practice program on fractions that awards badges and points. The dashboard on Ms. Grant's screen shows green, yellow, and red dots for each student. Ms. Grant is at her desk updating the grade book.

6When you ask a student what he's learning, he says, "Fractions. I'm on level 12." When you ask him to explain why 3/4 is bigger than 2/3, he says, "I don't know, I just click until it's right, and then it gives me the next one." Two students in the back have headphones in and are on the same screen they were on when you arrived.

Handout C for Look-Fors That Look for Learning

Starter Look-For Rubric

NameDate

A starting point for revision, not a finished tool. Each criterion should be scorable only by seeing or hearing student work or talk, with or without devices.

CriterionNot yet seenEmergingEvidentStrong
Purpose of the taskStudents can't say what they're learning, only what they're doing or which tool they're using.Some students can name the learning goal when asked.Most students can name the learning goal and how the task helps them reach it.Students explain the goal and why they chose a tool, format, or approach (including no tool) to reach it.
Student thinking made visibleWork shows answers only; no reasoning is visible or audible.Some reasoning is visible, mostly prompted by the teacher.Students explain or show reasoning in talk, writing, annotation, or revision.Students revise their thinking and can say what changed it.
Verification of information and AI outputInformation or AI output is accepted as given.Students check some information when prompted.Students question sources or AI output and check claims against other sources.Students name how they verified, what they rejected or corrected, and why.
Ownership and choiceAll students follow the same steps with no decisions to make.Students make minor choices (order, format).Students make meaningful decisions about approach, sources, or product.Students set goals, choose approaches, and reflect on whether their choices worked.
Access and inclusionSome students cannot participate fully (language, accessibility, device, or connection barriers are visible).Supports exist but not all students who need them are using them.Supports (language, accessibility, alternatives) are in use and students participate fully.Students use supports independently and the task offers more than one way to show learning.
